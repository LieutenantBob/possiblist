There is a cognitive bias so well-documented that it has its own name in multiple languages, and yet it continues to shape energy policy in every industrialised nation on Earth. It is the tendency to fear things that are dramatic, visible, and rare — and to ignore things that are mundane, invisible, and constant.

Coal-fired power plants kill approximately 24.6 people for every terawatt-hour of electricity they produce, through a combination of air pollution, mining accidents, and respiratory disease. Nuclear power kills 0.03 people per terawatt-hour. That is an eight-hundred-and-twenty-fold difference. If you were to rank energy sources by actual danger to human life, nuclear would sit at the very bottom of the table, alongside wind and solar. Coal would sit at the top, alone, by a margin that beggars belief.

And yet, when asked which energy source is most dangerous, a majority of people in every surveyed country say nuclear. This is not a small error. It is not a matter of degree. It is the complete inversion of reality, applied to a question that determines whether we burn the planet or not.

The reasons are not mysterious. They involve a reactor in Ukraine, a tsunami in Japan, and the human brain's deep, ancient, entirely understandable terror of invisible, involuntary, potentially catastrophic risks. But understanding the reasons does not change the consequences.

The psychology of nuclear fear is genuinely fascinating, and it tells us as much about the human mind as it does about energy policy.

In 1986, the Chernobyl disaster released a cloud of radioactive material across Europe. The immediate death toll was 31 — plant workers and firefighters. The long-term toll, estimated by the WHO and IAEA, is approximately 4,000 additional cancer deaths over several decades, primarily among the most heavily exposed populations. This is a genuine tragedy. It is also, in the context of global energy deaths, a footnote.

In the same period, coal-fired power plants killed approximately eight million people worldwide through air pollution alone. Eight million. This figure does not make the news because the deaths happen one at a time, in hospitals, attributed to "respiratory illness" or "heart disease" rather than to the power station upwind.

The psychologist Paul Slovic has spent decades studying why we fear nuclear more than coal, and his findings are elegant in their clarity. Humans assess risk not by calculating probabilities but by evaluating a set of qualitative characteristics. We fear risks that are involuntary (we did not choose to live near a reactor), catastrophic (many deaths at once rather than one at a time), unfamiliar (radiation is invisible), and dreaded (the word "nuclear" triggers associations with weapons). Coal possesses none of these characteristics. It is familiar, voluntary, chronic, and boring. It is also vastly more deadly.

The consequence of this perception gap is not merely academic. Germany's decision to close its nuclear plants after Fukushima led to increased reliance on coal and natural gas, producing an estimated additional twelve hundred deaths per year from air pollution. This is the lethal cost of good intentions guided by bad risk assessment.

France, which generates roughly seventy per cent of its electricity from nuclear power, has among the lowest carbon emissions per capita in Europe and some of the cheapest electricity. It also has one of the lowest rates of energy-related deaths. This is not a coincidence. It is the predictable consequence of choosing the safest option and sticking with it, against public sentiment.

The nuclear waste problem is real and unresolved. Spent fuel remains radioactive for thousands of years, and no country has yet opened a permanent geological repository (Finland's Onkalo facility is closest). This is a genuine challenge. It is also a challenge that affects a comparatively small volume of material, contained in known locations, under continuous monitoring. Compare this to the waste output of coal: millions of tons of CO2, particulate matter, mercury, and arsenic, dispersed invisibly into the atmosphere, monitored by nobody.
