The universe, for all its apparent indifference to human affairs, does occasionally arrange things with a sense of irony so precise that one suspects it of having read a script in advance. Consider the following arrangement: a species evolves on a small, damp planet, develops the ability to split atoms, immediately uses this ability to build weapons capable of ending civilisation, and then -- having terrified itself thoroughly -- decides that the single safest application of this technology is the one it cannot abide. The weapons, it keeps. The power plants, it protests. The species, it must be said, has a complicated relationship with consistency.

Somewhere around sixty-two per cent of people, when surveyed across multiple countries, identify nuclear power as the most dangerous source of electricity. This is not an unreasonable thing to believe. It is, in fact, an almost perfectly understandable thing to believe. It is also, by a margin that defies easy comprehension, wrong.

I believed it myself for years. Not in the way one believes a theorem or a creed, but in the quiet, unexamined way one believes that sharks are dangerous and that libraries are safe -- a background assumption so deeply embedded that it never occurred to me to check it. I suspect you believed it too, or something very close to it, and I suspect that if you are honest about it, you will find that this belief arrived not through evidence but through a series of images: a concrete sarcophagus in Ukraine, a wave hitting a Japanese coastline, a word -- *nuclear* -- that carries within it the accumulated dread of half a century of Cold War nightmares. The images are powerful. The data tells a different story.

Coal-fired power plants kill approximately 24.6 people for every terawatt-hour of electricity they produce. Nuclear power kills 0.03 people per terawatt-hour. If you are not accustomed to thinking in terawatt-hours -- and there is no particular reason you should be -- consider it this way: for every single person killed by nuclear power, coal kills eight hundred and twenty. This is not a rounding difference. It is not a matter of competing methodologies or disputed datasets. It is the kind of gap you could see from orbit. If energy sources were airlines, coal would have been grounded in 1952, and nuclear would have the safety record of the escalator at your local department store.

And yet here we are, building coal plants and closing nuclear ones, in a civilisation that has access to the data and has simply decided, collectively, not to look at it.

The reasons for this are not a mystery. They are, in fact, among the most thoroughly studied phenomena in the entire field of risk psychology, and they reveal something about the human mind that is at once deeply unflattering and entirely forgivable. But the consequences of this particular cognitive error are not measured in embarrassment. They are measured in approximately eight hundred thousand deaths per year.

Which is where the story stops being merely interesting and starts being rather urgent.

## The Three Accidents That Broke Our Thinking

To understand why we fear the safest thing, you need to understand three events. Not because they are unimportant -- they are genuinely significant -- but because the gap between what actually happened and what most people believe happened is, in each case, so vast that it constitutes a kind of secondary disaster all on its own.

**Chernobyl, 1986.** Ask someone how many people died at Chernobyl, and the answers you receive will range from "thousands" to "hundreds of thousands" to a vague but confident "millions." The actual figures, compiled by the World Health Organisation and the International Atomic Energy Agency after decades of study, are as follows: 31 people died in the immediate aftermath -- plant workers and firefighters, many of whom walked into lethal radiation fields with a courage that deserves more recognition than it typically receives. The estimated long-term toll from additional cancer deaths among the most heavily exposed populations is approximately 4,000 over several decades. The WHO's full assessment, including a broader population, places the upper estimate at around 9,000 additional cancer deaths over a lifetime. This is a genuine tragedy. It is also, when placed alongside the death toll of the energy source it is perpetually compared to, a figure that occupies a different order of magnitude from the one most people carry in their heads. A 2015 survey found that the median estimate of Chernobyl deaths among European respondents was approximately 40,000. The actual figure is smaller by a factor of roughly four to ten. The perception and the reality are not in the same postcode.

**Three Mile Island, 1979.** The partial meltdown at Three Mile Island in Pennsylvania is the worst nuclear accident in American history. The death toll was zero. The radiation released was, according to the Nuclear Regulatory Commission's own assessment, approximately one millirem above background levels for the surrounding population -- less than the radiation exposure from a single chest X-ray, and considerably less than the exposure one receives from eating approximately forty bananas. (We will return to the bananas shortly.) The health impact, after more than four decades of follow-up studies, remains: zero detectable excess cancers. The political impact was the effective end of new nuclear construction in the United States for thirty years. An accident that killed no one and injured no one shut down an industry. There is a word for this in psychology, and it is not "rational."

**Fukushima, 2011.** The Fukushima Daiichi disaster was triggered by the largest earthquake in Japan's recorded history, followed by a tsunami that killed approximately 18,500 people. The nuclear accident itself -- the meltdowns, the evacuations, the global media coverage that lasted months -- killed one person from radiation exposure. One. A single worker, whose death from lung cancer was attributed to radiation in 2018, seven years after the event. The evacuation, however, killed roughly 2,300 people, most of them elderly residents whose health deteriorated during forced relocation. The evacuation killed more people than the thing they were being evacuated from, by a ratio of approximately two thousand three hundred to one. If irony were a fuel source, this fact alone could power a small city.

There is a pattern here, and it is worth stating plainly. In the entire seven-decade history of civilian nuclear power, across every country that has operated reactors, the total death toll from radiation -- including Chernobyl, including Fukushima, including every minor incident ever recorded -- is somewhere in the range of 5,000 to 10,000 people, depending on which long-term estimates you accept. This is a real number. It represents real suffering. It is also fewer people than coal kills every four days.

## The Invisible Catastrophe

Coal does not kill people in dramatic, filmable ways. It does not explode. It does not glow. It does not require evacuation zones or hazmat suits or international emergency protocols. What it does is release fine particulate matter, sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, mercury, arsenic, and a rather impressive assortment of other substances into the air, continuously, around the clock, from every coal-fired power station on the planet.

The result, according to a comprehensive assessment published in The Lancet, is approximately 800,000 premature deaths per year from coal-generated air pollution alone. Not from all fossil fuels. Not from all air pollution. From coal power specifically. To put this in the terms we have been using: coal produces another Chernobyl roughly every two days. It has been doing this for decades. It does not make the evening news because the deaths arrive one at a time, in hospitals, attributed to "chronic obstructive pulmonary disease" or "ischaemic heart disease" or "lower respiratory infection" rather than to the power station upwind that has been gently poisoning the surrounding population since before most of them were born.

And here is the thing that really stops you in your tracks, the fact from which the entire absurdity of our energy fears becomes impossible to ignore: coal power plants release more radioactive material into the environment than nuclear power plants do. This is not a provocation. It is a measurement. Coal contains trace amounts of uranium, thorium, and their radioactive decay products. When you burn coal, these materials are concentrated in the fly ash and released into the atmosphere. A 1978 paper in *Science* by J.P. McBride and colleagues at Oak Ridge National Laboratory calculated that the radiation exposure to populations living near coal plants was, in some cases, higher than the exposure to populations living near nuclear plants. The coal simply has the good manners to distribute its radiation gradually, invisibly, and without the courtesy of a warning siren, which is apparently sufficient to make it socially acceptable.

## Why the Brain Gets This Wrong

The psychologist Paul Slovic has spent the better part of five decades studying how humans evaluate risk, and his findings are, for anyone who hoped our species made decisions based on evidence, somewhat deflating.

Humans do not assess risk by calculating probabilities. We assess risk by consulting a mental inventory of qualitative characteristics that have almost nothing to do with actual danger and almost everything to do with how a risk *feels*. Slovic, along with Baruch Fischhoff and Sarah Lichtenstein, identified the key dimensions in a landmark 1980 paper: we fear risks that are *involuntary* (imposed on us rather than chosen), *catastrophic* (many deaths at once rather than spread over time), *unfamiliar* (poorly understood by the affected population), *dreaded* (carrying an element of particular horror), and *uncontrollable* (feeling that neither the individual nor the authorities can prevent the outcome).

Nuclear power scores high on every single one of these dimensions. It is imposed on communities. It can, in theory, produce mass casualties. Radiation is invisible and poorly understood. The word "nuclear" triggers associations with weapons, mushroom clouds, and the end of civilisation. And the consequences feel uncontrollable -- you cannot see radiation, smell it, taste it, or run away from it in any direction that seems meaningful.

Coal scores low on all of them. Coal is familiar. Coal is associated with industry, warmth, employment, heritage. Coal pollution kills slowly and individually, which the human brain categorises as a fundamentally different kind of threat from a sudden catastrophe, even when the cumulative toll is orders of magnitude larger. Daniel Kahneman, whose work on cognitive biases won him the Nobel Prize in Economics, identified this as a manifestation of the availability heuristic: we judge the likelihood and severity of events by how easily we can imagine them. A nuclear meltdown is vivid, cinematic, terrifying. Coal pollution is grey, diffuse, and boring. One makes for a Hollywood blockbuster. The other makes for, at best, a paragraph on page fourteen of a WHO report.

This is what Slovic calls "dread risk" -- the special category of fear reserved for hazards that are perceived as uncontrollable and potentially catastrophic, regardless of their actual probability. It is the reason people fear flying more than driving, despite the fact that driving is, by every available measure, vastly more dangerous. It is the reason a shark attack in Florida makes international news while the roughly 1.35 million annual traffic deaths worldwide do not. And it is the reason a form of energy that has killed fewer people in its entire history than coal kills in a single week continues to be treated as though it were the greater danger.

## The Country That Quietly Got It Right

France generates approximately seventy-eight per cent of its electricity from nuclear power. It has done so since the 1980s, when the Messmer Plan -- launched in response to the 1973 oil crisis by Prime Minister Pierre Messmer, who made the decision with a speed and decisiveness that modern democracies would find frankly alarming -- built fifty-six reactors in roughly fifteen years. The result is that France has among the lowest carbon emissions per unit of electricity in Europe, some of the cheapest electricity on the continent, and an energy-related death rate that is, by any reasonable standard, negligibly small.

Nobody talks about this. Germany, which generates roughly a third of its electricity from coal and has spent the better part of two decades closing nuclear plants in response to public anxiety following Fukushima, is discussed constantly. Germany's Energiewende -- its celebrated energy transition -- has cost over 500 billion euros, produced a grid that remains significantly more carbon-intensive than France's, and resulted in an estimated additional 1,100 to 1,200 deaths per year from air pollution attributable to the increased reliance on coal and natural gas that filled the gap left by nuclear closures. This is the lethal arithmetic of good intentions guided by dread rather than data.

France, meanwhile, sits next door, producing electricity that is both cleaner and cheaper, with a safety record that has produced precisely zero radiation-related deaths in the civilian programme's entire history, and is studied by energy economists around the world as the most successful large-scale decarbonisation of an electricity grid ever achieved. The response of the global public to this information has been, by and large, to continue not knowing about it.

## The Disaster Nobody Has Heard Of

In August 1975, Typhoon Nina struck the Banqiao Dam and sixty-one other dams in China's Henan province. The Banqiao Dam, a hydroelectric facility, failed catastrophically. The resulting flood -- a wall of water ten kilometres wide and several metres deep, travelling at roughly fifty kilometres per hour -- killed approximately 26,000 people immediately. The subsequent famine and disease brought the total death toll to an estimated 170,000, with some estimates reaching 240,000. It remains the deadliest energy-related disaster in human history. It was not nuclear. It was hydropower.

Ask a reasonably well-informed person to name the deadliest energy disaster in history, and they will say Chernobyl. Chernobyl's total estimated death toll, at the highest credible estimate, is less than five per cent of Banqiao's. But Banqiao happened in China in 1975, in a country that did not widely report the disaster for decades, and it involved water rather than radiation. Water is familiar. Water is not dreaded. The Banqiao Dam collapse killed more than forty times as many people as Chernobyl, and it occupies approximately none of the space in public consciousness that Chernobyl occupies.

This is not a failure of education. It is a feature of the human risk-assessment apparatus working exactly as evolved, which is to say: working in a way that was superbly adapted to the African savannah seventy thousand years ago and is rather less suited to evaluating comparative energy-source mortality in the twenty-first century.

## The Banana Equivalent Dose

There is a unit of radiation measurement that tells you nearly everything you need to know about the actual scale of nuclear risk as experienced by ordinary humans. It is the Banana Equivalent Dose.

Bananas contain potassium-40, a naturally occurring radioactive isotope. Eating one banana exposes you to approximately 0.1 microsieverts of radiation. This is a real number with a real unit, and it provides a useful scale for understanding radiation exposures that are otherwise described in units that mean nothing to most people.

The average radiation exposure from living within fifty miles of a nuclear power plant for one year is approximately 0.09 microsieverts. This is less than one banana. The average exposure from the Three Mile Island accident to the surrounding population was roughly 80 microsieverts, or the equivalent of eating about 800 bananas, which is to say about two years' worth of bananas for someone with a reasonably serious banana habit. The annual radiation exposure from natural background sources -- cosmic rays, radon in your basement, the potassium in your own bones -- is roughly 3,000 microsieverts, or 30,000 bananas. You irradiate yourself more by existing in a house with a granite countertop than by living next to a nuclear plant.

None of this is to say that large radiation doses are not dangerous. They are. Acutely lethal doses start at around 4,000,000 microsieverts, or the equivalent of forty million bananas, which is, one feels, more bananas than any reasonable person should be asked to contemplate. But the gap between the radiation levels that actually exist around nuclear facilities and the radiation levels that would cause harm is not a gap. It is a chasm so wide that the two sides cannot see each other.

## The Complications That Are Real

A possiblist does not dismiss legitimate concerns, and nuclear energy has them.

The waste problem is genuine. Spent nuclear fuel remains radioactive for thousands of years, and as of this writing, no country has opened a permanent deep geological repository, though Finland's Onkalo facility -- a tunnel bored into 1.8-billion-year-old bedrock on the island of Olkiluoto -- is expected to begin operations within the decade. The total volume of high-level nuclear waste ever produced by the entire global nuclear industry in its seventy-year history would fit in a structure roughly the size of a football pitch, stacked to a height of about ten metres. This is a surprisingly small amount of material. It is also a surprisingly dangerous amount of material, and "surprisingly small but surprisingly dangerous" is not a combination that resolves itself neatly.

The proliferation risk is real. The same enrichment technology that produces reactor fuel can, with modification, produce weapons-grade material. This is not a theoretical concern. It is the reason the international community maintains an elaborate and imperfect system of inspections and agreements, and it is the reason every serious proposal for expanding nuclear energy must grapple with questions of governance that have no purely technical answer.

The cost overruns are spectacular. The Hinkley Point C reactor in Somerset, originally budgeted at 18 billion pounds, is now expected to cost between 31 and 34 billion, with completion dates that have migrated across the calendar like a particularly indecisive appointment. The Vogtle expansion in Georgia, the first new nuclear construction in the United States in decades, came in at roughly twice its original budget and seven years late. These are not trivial problems. An energy source that is safe and clean but cannot be built on time or on budget has a credibility problem that no amount of favourable mortality statistics will resolve.

These are real challenges. They deserve real engagement. What they do not do, individually or collectively, is change the fundamental arithmetic: the thing we fear is the safest option on the table, and the thing we accept without much thought is the one that is killing us.

## The Quiet Close

There is something both humbling and clarifying about discovering that your fear has been pointing in the wrong direction. Not slightly wrong. Not wrong in a way that a reasonable person might defend. Wrong by a factor of eight hundred and twenty, applied to a question on which hundreds of thousands of lives depend every year.

The species has a choice, and it is not a complicated one. It is merely one that requires looking at a spreadsheet instead of a movie reel, consulting a mortality table instead of a feeling, and accepting that the thing which frightens us and the thing which harms us are, in this case, not merely different but almost perfectly inverted.

France noticed. Most of the world has not.

The data is patient. It will wait.
