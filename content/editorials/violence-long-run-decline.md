The human species has spent approximately three hundred thousand years refining its capacity for violence. It has invented the spear, the sword, the crossbow, the cannon, the machine gun, the cruise missile, and a device that can eliminate a city in the time it takes to boil a kettle. It has fought wars over land, gold, religion, honour, salt, a stolen bucket, and, on at least one occasion, a pig that wandered across an international border. Given this record, one might reasonably conclude that humans are getting worse at coexisting with each other and that the project of civilisation is, on the whole, a qualified failure.

One would, with considerable confidence, be wrong.

When Possiblist's pilot survey asked people whether the average person today is more or less likely to die by violence than someone living six hundred years ago, sixty-seven per cent chose the wrong answer. The most popular wrong answer was "much more likely," which is not merely incorrect but incorrect in a direction so emphatic that it constitutes a kind of achievement. Two out of three humans, given the choice between reality and the precise opposite of reality, chose the opposite. Confidently. This is not an insult to two-thirds of the species. I was in that two-thirds. Most people reading this were probably in that two-thirds. It is a reasonable thing to believe, given everything we see on television and in our feeds and on the notifications that illuminate our phones seventeen times an hour with news of the latest atrocity. It just happens to be spectacularly, measurably, documentably untrue.

In medieval Europe, the homicide rate ran somewhere around fifty per hundred thousand people per year. In some times and places, it was considerably higher -- estimates for fourteenth-century Oxford, a city ostensibly dedicated to learning, reach as high as a hundred and ten per hundred thousand, which gives "town and gown tensions" a somewhat different complexion than the phrase currently implies. Today, the global average is approximately six per hundred thousand. In western Europe, it is roughly one. The decline is not a rounding error. It is not a statistical artefact produced by creative accounting. It is a drop of somewhere between thirty-fold and a hundred-fold, depending on where you measure, sustained over six centuries, visible in every data set anyone has ever assembled on the subject.

A newspaper could have run the headline "FEWER PEOPLE WERE MURDERED TODAY THAN ON THIS DATE IN 1400" every single day for six hundred years and it would have been, on trend, accurate. It has never been a headline. This is not because editors are conspiring to hide good news. It is because a slow, structural decline in violence over centuries is, by every formal criterion of newsworthiness, the least reportable thing that can happen. It happened anyway.

How it happened -- and why it does not feel like it happened -- is a story that involves a Harvard psychologist with eight hundred pages to spare, a German sociologist who noticed something peculiar about table manners, and a feature of your brain that was useful on the savannah and is rather less useful in an information environment designed to sell your attention by alarming you.

## A Walk Through Medieval London, If You Dare

The best way to appreciate the decline of violence is to take a walk through a medieval city, which is a thing you should do only in your imagination and with a strong stomach.

London in the fourteenth century had a population of roughly fifty thousand and a homicide rate that historians estimate at thirty-six to fifty-two per hundred thousand -- roughly ten times the rate of modern-day Baltimore, a city that Americans routinely describe as dangerous. The streets were narrow, unlit, and patrolled by nobody in particular. The concept of a professional police force would not arrive for another five hundred years. Disputes were settled by the parties involved, which meant they were frequently settled with knives. Coroners' rolls from the period record an astonishing range of causes of death by violence: stabbings in taverns, beatings over commercial disputes, killings arising from insults to honour, and a surprising number of drownings that may or may not have been accidents, depending on how charitable one feels toward the deceased's drinking companions.

Going to a feast was, statistically, a higher-risk activity than walking through most modern cities after midnight. The cutlery was less decorative and rather more frequently weaponised. Arguments over seating arrangements could escalate to bloodshed with a speed that would strike a modern dinner party guest as, at minimum, an overreaction. The German sociologist Norbert Elias noticed something about this. In his 1939 work *The Civilizing Process*, he traced the slow, centuries-long shift in European manners and self-control -- from a medieval world where grown men routinely urinated at the dinner table, blew their noses on the tablecloth, and stabbed each other over perceived slights, to a modern world where people queue politely for coffee and experience genuine distress if someone fails to use a coaster. Elias argued that this was not mere etiquette. It was the outward expression of a profound psychological transformation: the internalisation of self-restraint, the growing capacity to regulate impulse, the slow replacement of honour-based violence with institutional dispute resolution. The fork, in this reading, was not just a utensil. It was a civilisational technology.

This is the kind of thesis that sounds absurd until you notice how much evidence supports it. The decline of violence in Europe maps almost exactly onto the period of increasing table manners, expanding literacy, and the emergence of centralised states that claimed a monopoly on the legitimate use of force. Which brings us to the Harvard psychologist.

## The Man with Eight Hundred Pages

Steven Pinker published *The Better Angels of Our Nature* in 2011. It is eight hundred and thirty-two pages long, which is either a sign of thoroughness or a sign that the editor was on holiday, depending on your relationship with long books. Its central argument is simple, and its supporting evidence is, in the technical sense, overwhelming: violence has declined at every scale, over every timescale, in virtually every measurable category, for centuries.

Not just homicide. War deaths per capita have fallen. Deaths in battle as a proportion of the global population have fallen. Torture, which was once a spectator sport in European town squares, has been abolished in most countries and hidden in the rest, which is not the same as eliminated but is, in Pinker's framing, an improvement in norms even where practice lags behind. Slavery, which was once so universal it was barely remarked upon, is now illegal everywhere on Earth, even in the places where it persists. Executions, animal cruelty, domestic violence -- all trending downward over the long arc, even where progress is painfully slow and unevenly distributed.

Pinker identifies several forces driving the decline. The first is the Leviathan -- Thomas Hobbes's term for the state. When a central authority claims the exclusive right to use force and backs that claim with courts, laws, and a standing army, the cost of private violence goes up dramatically. You can still stab someone over a land dispute, but now the state will find you and punish you, which introduces a calculus that medieval freelancers did not face. The homicide rate in Europe began its steepest decline precisely during the period when feudal fragmentation gave way to centralised nation-states. This is not a coincidence.

The second force is commerce. Trade creates a situation in which your neighbour's continued existence is more profitable than their death. A peasant in 1350 could raid the next village for grain and face minimal consequences. A merchant in 1650 who raided his trading partners would find his supply chain in ruins. The economist's phrase for this is "positive-sum interaction," which is a clinical way of describing the moment when humans worked out that cooperation is more lucrative than plunder. It is, when stated plainly, remarkable that the species needed several thousand years to arrive at this conclusion, but it did arrive, and the world got measurably less stabby as a result.

The third force is what Pinker, borrowing from the philosopher Peter Singer, calls the "expanding circle" of empathy. Over centuries, the boundary of who counts as fully human -- whose suffering matters, whose death requires justification -- has expanded outward from family to clan to tribe to nation to, in principle, all of humanity. This expansion is incomplete, inconsistent, and frequently hypocritical. But a medieval lord who tortured a prisoner for entertainment was operating within his society's norms. A modern government that tortures prisoners must deny it is doing so, which means the norm has shifted even where behaviour has not. Hypocrisy, as La Rochefoucauld noted, is the tribute vice pays to virtue. In the context of violence, it is also evidence that the definition of acceptable behaviour has narrowed considerably.

## Why It Feels Like the Opposite

Here is where a possiblist holds two facts in each hand and does not drop either one.

The decline of violence is real. The perception that violence is increasing is also real. Both things are true simultaneously, which is uncomfortable but not contradictory.

The perception exists for reasons that are not stupid. The twenty-four-hour news cycle is a machine optimised for salience, and violence is the most salient thing there is. A camera phone in every pocket means that acts of violence that would have gone unrecorded in 1990 are now filmed, uploaded, shared, commented upon, and algorithmically amplified to the feeds of millions. The availability heuristic -- Daniel Kahneman's term for the tendency to judge the frequency of events by how easily examples come to mind -- means that a single vivid shooting on the evening news does more to shape your sense of risk than a thousand pages of declining homicide statistics. Your brain evolved on the savannah, where the rustle in the grass might be a predator and the cost of ignoring it was death. The cost of overestimating threats was merely anxiety. We are the descendants of the anxious ones. The relaxed ones were eaten.

This is not a design flaw. It is a feature that kept your ancestors alive and now keeps you misinformed. The information environment has changed; the hardware running your threat-detection software has not.

Max Roser of Our World in Data has put this with characteristic precision: the news reports on events, not on trends. A country that has a civil war is news. A country that might have had a civil war and did not is never news. The quiet, structural absence of violence is, by definition, invisible.

## The Honest Complications

And now for the part where the chart gets more complicated, because it does, and pretending otherwise would be the kind of selective cheerfulness that gives progress a bad name.

Syria. Between 2011 and 2023, an estimated three hundred and fifty thousand to six hundred thousand people were killed in a civil war that displaced half the country's population. Yemen. A war that has killed over a hundred and fifty thousand people directly and hundreds of thousands more through famine and disease, in a conflict that most of the world has struggled to locate on a map. Ethiopia's Tigray region. Myanmar. Sudan. The long-run decline of violence is a trend, not a guarantee, and the people living inside the exceptions are not comforted by the trend line.

Domestic violence remains endemic in every country on Earth, including the ones with the lowest homicide rates. An estimated one in three women globally has experienced physical or sexual violence from an intimate partner. This is a category of violence that the historical data largely misses, because it happened inside homes and was not recorded, which means the long-run decline may be partly a story about which kinds of violence became visible enough to measure.

Mass shootings in the United States occupy a peculiar statistical position: they are rare enough to be insignificant in the aggregate data and frequent enough to dominate the national conversation about violence. This is not a contradiction. It is a demonstration of exactly how the availability heuristic works. Each event is horrific, vivid, and extensively covered. The aggregate homicide rate in the United States has declined by roughly half since 1990. Both facts are true. Holding them simultaneously requires the kind of discomfort that a possiblist does not try to resolve, because resolving it in either direction would require discarding evidence.

Nuclear weapons introduce a category of risk that has no historical precedent. The long decline of violence was achieved by a species that could, for most of its history, only kill people one at a time, or at most one army at a time. A species that can eliminate a city in four seconds has not abolished violence. It has merely raised the stakes of failure to a level that makes the medieval homicide rate seem, in retrospect, quaint. The fact that nuclear weapons have not been used since 1945 is either a triumph of deterrence or eighty years of luck, and nobody is entirely certain which.

## The Long Arc

Here is what the data says, stated as plainly as it can be stated.

You are roughly eight times less likely to die by violence than a person born six hundred years ago. This is true despite the twenty-four-hour news cycle, despite mass shootings, despite the wars in Syria and Yemen and Sudan. It is true not because those things are unimportant but because the baseline from which we started was so extraordinarily violent that even with all of the horrors of the modern world, the trajectory is unmistakably downward.

The forces that drove the decline -- the state monopoly on violence, the expansion of trade, the slow widening of the circle of who counts as human -- are not natural laws. They are institutions, norms, and habits, and they can be dismantled. The trend is not destiny. It is an achievement, and achievements require maintenance.

Sixty-seven per cent of people believe the world is more violent than ever. They are wrong, but they are wrong for understandable reasons, and being wrong about this particular fact has consequences. If you believe the world is getting more violent, you are more likely to support policies rooted in fear. If you know it has gotten less violent, you might ask a different kind of question: not "how do we survive?" but "how do we continue?"

The medieval banquet guest who stabbed his neighbour over a seating arrangement would not recognise the world we live in. We queue for coffee. We argue on the internet. We experience genuine outrage when someone cuts in line.

The data does not say we are good. It says we are better. That is enough to be worth noticing.
