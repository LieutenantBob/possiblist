Humanity has been organising itself into groups with leaders for roughly ten thousand years, give or take whatever you think was happening in Mesopotamia. For approximately ninety-nine point seven per cent of that time, the job description for "person in charge" included, implicitly but unmistakably, a requirement that the applicant not be a woman. This was not written down, which made it more durable than if it had been, because written rules can be repealed and unwritten ones simply persist, like damp.

Then, in 1960, something happened in what was then called Ceylon. A woman became the leader of a country. Not a queen — queens had been running things on and off for centuries, though usually by accident of birth and frequently while people complained about it. A prime minister. Elected. Democratically. By people who could have chosen someone else and chose her instead.

Her name was Sirimavo Bandaranaike. If you have not heard of her, you are in excellent and extensive company. Sixty-four per cent of people, when asked how many countries have ever had a woman as head of state or government, guess about thirty-five. I would have guessed something similar, and I am supposedly paying attention. The availability heuristic is running the show: you do a quick mental inventory — Thatcher, Merkel, maybe Jacinda Ardern — and then extrapolate from a sample that turns out to be wildly unrepresentative of the actual total.

The actual total is around seventy.

This is either more or fewer than you expected, and whichever it is reveals something interesting about your assumptions. Seventy countries have, at some point since Mrs Bandaranaike took office in Colombo, been led by a woman. That is more than a third of the world's nations. It has been happening, incrementally and without much fanfare, for over six decades, and the popular mental model has not caught up. The gap between the number in your head and the number on the page is itself a small piece of data about how progress works: slowly enough that nobody updates their priors, and then someone writes it down and it looks, briefly, astonishing.

What is genuinely astonishing is the geography. This is not, as many people vaguely assume, a phenomenon confined to Scandinavia and the occasional Anglophone anomaly. Bangladesh has had women as prime minister for most of the past thirty years — Sheikh Hasina and Khaleda Zia traded the office back and forth with a regularity that would have been remarkable even without the gender dimension. Liberia elected Ellen Johnson Sirleaf, Africa's first woman president, in 2005. Latin America has had multiple: Chile, Argentina, Brazil, Costa Rica, Bolivia, Honduras, Peru. South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Nepal, the Philippines, Indonesia, Myanmar. The list, once you start compiling it, has the quality of a thing that should have been more famous.

And then there are the absences, which are at least as interesting as the presences.

## The Dog That Did Not Bark

The United States, as of this writing, has never had a woman president. Neither has Russia, though this is perhaps less surprising given that Russia's recent approach to political transition has not been what you would call open to experimentation. Japan has never had a woman prime minister. Italy managed it for the first time only in 2022, with Giorgia Meloni, and the circumstances were complicated enough that they probably deserve their own paragraph, which they will get shortly.

These absences are not random. The countries that have never had a woman at the top tend to fall into two categories: those with political systems so consolidated around a single figure or party that the pipeline question never arises, and those with political systems so apparently open and democratic that the absence becomes, stated plainly, striking. The United States has had forty-six presidents. All of them have been men. The probability of this occurring by chance, in a country where women are fifty-one per cent of the population and have been eligible for the presidency for over a century, is the kind of number that makes statisticians put down their coffee.

## The First and the Forgotten

Sirimavo Bandaranaike deserves rather more attention than history has given her, which is almost none. She became prime minister of Ceylon in 1960, following the assassination of her husband, who had held the job before her. The "widow's path to power" is a pattern that recurs with uncomfortable frequency in the early decades of women's political leadership — Indira Gandhi, Corazon Aquino, Benazir Bhutto — and it raises a question that scholars have been arguing about since: does it matter how you got there, or does it matter that you got there?

Bandaranaike served three terms. She nationalised industries, pursued a non-aligned foreign policy, oversaw the country's transition from Ceylon to Sri Lanka, and was stripped of her civic rights for abuse of power in 1980. She was, in other words, a complete politician — capable of vision and overreach in roughly equal measure — which is exactly what equality looks like when you take the romanticism out of it. The expectation that women leaders should be better than their male counterparts, rather than equivalently competent and equivalently flawed, is itself a form of inequality so quiet that it barely registers.

Isabel Peron became president of Argentina in 1974 — the first woman to hold the title of president anywhere — and was overthrown by a military coup two years later. Vigdis Finnbogadottir won the presidency of Iceland in 1980, in part by campaigning explicitly as a single mother, which in 1980 was the kind of thing that was supposed to disqualify you. She won four terms. The world did not take the hint.

## The Bangladesh Paradox and the Geography of Surprise

Here is the thing that really gets you. Bangladesh, which ranks one hundred and forty-seventh out of one hundred and fifty-six countries on the World Economic Forum's gender gap index, has had a woman as head of government for most of the past three decades. The country has significant challenges in women's education, economic participation, and personal safety, and yet the top political job has been held by a woman more often than not. This is not a contradiction. It is a demonstration that representation at the top and equality throughout are different things, related but not synonymous, and conflating them is one of the ways the conversation goes wrong.

The pattern repeats. India elected Indira Gandhi in 1966, at a time when most Indian women could not open a bank account without their husband's permission. The Philippines elected Corazon Aquino in 1986 and Gloria Macapagal Arroyo in 2001. Pakistan elected Benazir Bhutto twice. In each case, the woman who reached the top came from a political dynasty — which tells you something about which doors were open and which were not. The door marked "daughter of a famous political family" was ajar. The door marked "woman with her own political career" was, in most of these countries, still locked.

## Did It Change Anything?

The research on whether women leaders govern differently is mixed, which is the academic way of saying it depends on what you measure and how much nuance you can tolerate.

A 2014 study in the *American Journal of Political Science* found that countries with women leaders had significantly lower disaster death rates — not because women leaders prevented disasters, but because they invested more in disaster preparedness and healthcare infrastructure. A study of Indian village councils, where a quota system randomly assigned women to leadership positions, found that women leaders spent more on water infrastructure and less on roads, which aligned more closely with the stated preferences of women in those villages. Whether "more water, fewer roads" constitutes better governance depends, rather obviously, on whether you need water or a road.

Research on peace negotiations presents an interesting pattern. When women participate in peace processes, the resulting agreements are thirty-five per cent more likely to last at least fifteen years, according to a study by the Council on Foreign Relations. This is a correlation that may or may not be causal, but it is the kind of number that should at least make you pause before dismissing the question.

Healthcare spending tends to be higher under women leaders. Education spending tends to be higher. Military spending tends to be — and this is where the data gets genuinely interesting — about the same. The notion that women leaders are inherently more peaceful is not well supported by the evidence. Indira Gandhi oversaw a nuclear weapons programme. Margaret Thatcher went to war over the Falklands. Golda Meir commanded the Israeli military during the Yom Kippur War. Women leaders, it turns out, are capable of the full range of political behaviour, including the parts that do not look good on an inspirational poster.

## The Rwanda Number

Rwanda's parliament is sixty-one per cent women. This is the highest proportion in the world. It happened after a genocide.

In 1994, the Rwandan genocide killed approximately eight hundred thousand people in a hundred days, predominantly men. In the aftermath, women constituted seventy per cent of the surviving population. They rebuilt. The 2003 constitution mandated that thirty per cent of parliamentary seats be held by women — a quota that the country then exceeded so dramatically that the quota became a floor, not a ceiling. By 2008, Rwanda became the first country in history where women held a majority of parliamentary seats.

This is not a comfortable story. The conditions that produced the world's most gender-balanced parliament were, by any measure, catastrophic. The progress is real. The context is horrifying. A possiblist holds both of these facts at the same time.

## The Glass Cliff, the Quota Debate, and the Pace

There is a phenomenon in organisational psychology called the glass cliff: women are disproportionately likely to be appointed to leadership positions during times of crisis, when the probability of failure is highest. The metaphor is precise — you break through the glass ceiling only to find yourself standing on a glass cliff. Teresa May inherited Brexit. Ellen Johnson Sirleaf inherited a country emerging from civil war. The question of whether this pattern reflects a genuine willingness to try new approaches during desperate times or a quieter willingness to let women take the blame for situations that were already failing is, as the researchers say, unresolved.

The quota debate is similarly unresolved and similarly important. Rwanda, Bolivia, Mexico, and the United Arab Emirates have all implemented quotas with varying designs and varying results. The strongest evidence suggests that quotas do increase women's representation and that the "quality" concern — that quotas will produce less competent legislators — is not supported by the data. A study of Swedish municipal councils found that quotas increased the competence of the overall political class by displacing mediocre men, which is a sentence that deserves to be read twice.

And yet. At the current rate of change, the World Economic Forum estimates it will take one hundred and thirty-one years to close the global gender gap in political empowerment. At that pace, gender parity in political leadership would arrive around the year 2157, which is also, by coincidence, roughly when several optimistic projections suggest humans might have a permanent settlement on Mars. It is not clear which milestone is more likely.

## The Nuance That Matters

The cumulative count — seventy countries — is higher than most people expect. But the snapshot is less encouraging. At any given moment, only about ten to fifteen per cent of the world's countries have a woman in the top job. Many of the tenures on the list were brief. Some were ceremonial. Several were authoritarian. Listing Indira Gandhi's emergency rule alongside Jacinda Ardern's pandemic response as equivalent examples of "women's leadership" requires a definition of the category so broad that it ceases to be analytically useful. The question of what counts as "real" representation — symbolic versus substantive, dynastic versus independent, elected versus appointed — is more contested than the headline number suggests.

There is also the pipeline problem. Women hold roughly twenty-seven per cent of parliamentary seats worldwide, up from eleven per cent in 1995. This is real progress. It is also the kind of progress that, if you drew it as a line on a graph and extended it forward, would not reach fifty per cent in the lifetime of anyone currently alive. The pipeline to leadership is, in the language of the researchers, spectacularly leaky. Women enter politics. They are elected to local councils, to legislatures, to cabinets. And then, somewhere between "promising minister" and "serious candidate for the top job," a disproportionate number of them stop advancing, for reasons that are structural, cultural, financial, and exhaustively documented.

## Seventy Countries and Counting

Sirimavo Bandaranaike took office in 1960. Since then, the list has grown by roughly one country per year, which is the kind of pace that is simultaneously real and inadequate. It is happening. It is not happening fast. The world has had sixty-six years to process the idea that women can run countries and has arrived, collectively, at "probably, under certain conditions, if the circumstances are right and nobody better is available." This is not a stirring assessment.

But the list exists, and it is longer than you thought. Seventy countries. Six continents. Every major religion and system of government represented. The evidence has been accumulating, quietly, in the way that most real progress accumulates — without a headline, without a parade, without anyone memorising the names.

Almost nobody knows who Sirimavo Bandaranaike was. Almost everybody lives in the world she helped, in some small way, to make possible.

The list is still being written.
