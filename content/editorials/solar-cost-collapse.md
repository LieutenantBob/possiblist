The universe is, by most accounts, approximately thirteen point eight billion years old, and for nearly all of that time it has been producing an astonishing amount of free energy by the straightforward method of fusing hydrogen into helium. The nearest star to this planet converts roughly six hundred million tonnes of hydrogen into helium every second, radiating the results in all directions with the cheerful indifference of a sprinkler on a Sunday lawn. It has been doing this for four and a half billion years. It will continue for another five billion or so. The energy arrives at the surface of the Earth at no charge, without a contract, and without requiring a drilling permit.

For most of recorded history, the dominant strategy of the species has been to ignore this and dig up dead things instead.

When Possiblist asked people how much the cost of solar energy had fallen since 2010, fifty-eight per cent got it wrong. This is, by the standards of human wrongness about global trends, almost respectable. Most people correctly intuited the direction -- solar got cheaper -- but guessed around fifty per cent. The actual figure is eighty-nine per cent. In 2010, generating a kilowatt-hour of electricity from sunlight cost about thirty-six cents. By 2024, it cost roughly four cents. This is not a gentle downward slope. This is the kind of cost collapse that, if it happened to the price of a car, would mean you could buy a Toyota Corolla for the price of a decent lunch.

I would have guessed fifty per cent myself, if I am honest, and I had read several articles about how solar was getting cheaper before sitting down to write this one. The human brain is not built to intuit exponential curves. We expect things to improve gradually, steadily, in the manner of a conscientious student progressing through school. What solar costs actually did was more like a conscientious student who suddenly remembered they could fly, pitched themselves off the roof, and landed in a different postcode entirely.

The number is so large that it changes the nature of the conversation. When something falls in price by eighty-nine per cent, you are no longer asking whether it can compete. You are asking why everything else is still so expensive. The sun, it turns out, has been running a rather aggressive pricing strategy, and virtually nobody in the forecasting business saw it coming.

That last part -- the forecasters -- is where the story becomes, if you have a certain disposition, genuinely hilarious. But it requires some history, a man named Swanson, a laboratory in New Jersey, and a chart of predictions so consistently wrong that it belongs in a museum of institutional humility.

## A Panel, a Price, and a Law Named After a Man Who Made Them

The story begins, as an unreasonable number of twentieth-century technology stories do, at Bell Labs in Murray Hill, New Jersey. In 1954, three researchers -- Daryl Chapin, Calvin Fuller, and Gerald Pearson -- demonstrated the first practical silicon photovoltaic cell. It converted about six per cent of the sunlight hitting it into electricity. The New York Times ran the story on page one and called it the beginning of "a new era."

The price of that first practical solar cell worked out to roughly seventy-six dollars per watt. To put this in context: generating enough electricity to run a single modern laptop would have cost, at 1954 prices, approximately five thousand dollars in panels alone. The sun's energy was free. Collecting it was roughly as economical as hiring a team of squirrels to generate power on miniature treadmills.

For the next two decades, the only customer who could justify the expense was the one customer for whom cost was not the primary concern: the space programme. Satellites need electricity, the sun is always shining in space, and there are no coal deliveries in low Earth orbit. Vanguard 1, launched in 1958, carried solar cells and is still up there, making it the oldest human-made object in orbit -- a small, silent, solar-powered sphere that has been circling the planet for sixty-eight years, long after everyone involved in building it has retired or died. It was designed to last a few years. The solar cells, apparently, did not get the memo.

What happened next is described by something called Swanson's Law, named after Richard Swanson, the founder of SunPower Corporation. Swanson observed in the early 2000s that the price of solar photovoltaic cells tends to drop by approximately twenty per cent for every doubling of cumulative shipped volume. This is solar energy's version of Moore's Law, the famous observation that the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years. The critical difference is that while Moore's Law describes a technology getting more powerful, Swanson's Law describes a technology getting cheaper. Both curves are exponential. Both curves are the kind of thing that humans are spectacularly bad at projecting forward in their heads, which is why virtually every serious forecast of solar deployment has been wrong, and wrong in the same direction, for a quarter of a century.

## The Most Consistently Wrong Chart in Energy Economics

The International Energy Agency publishes an annual World Energy Outlook. It is the most widely cited energy forecast on the planet. Governments use it to set policy. Corporations use it to plan investments. It is, by institutional reputation, the gold standard.

It has also been wrong about solar energy so consistently, so dramatically, and so persistently in one direction that the resulting chart -- showing IEA projections plotted against actual solar deployment -- has become something of an underground classic among energy analysts. It is the kind of chart that makes you laugh the first time you see it, and then makes you slightly concerned the second time, because these are the projections that governments use to plan the future.

Here is how it works. Each year, the IEA would project how much solar capacity the world would install over the coming decades. Each year, the world would blow past that projection within two to three years. The IEA would then revise their forecast upward. The world would blow past the revision. The IEA would revise again. The world would blow past again. This pattern repeated for roughly fifteen consecutive years. In some years, the IEA's projection for 2030 solar capacity was exceeded by actual deployment before 2020. They were not off by ten or twenty per cent. They were off by factors of ten. In some vintage years, by factors closer to a hundred.

This is not a criticism of the IEA's competence. It is an observation about the nature of exponential curves and the institutional difficulty of projecting them. Linear minds produce linear forecasts. Exponential phenomena make linear forecasters look foolish. The IEA's analysts were doing exactly what trained experts do: extrapolating from recent trends with reasonable adjustments. The problem is that "reasonable" and "exponential" are not on speaking terms. A reasonable person, looking at the solar cost curve in 2005 and asked to project forward, would have predicted gradual improvement. What actually happened was the economic equivalent of a rock falling off a cliff.

Auke Hoekstra, an energy researcher at Eindhoven University of Technology, has compiled these charts with what can only be described as affectionate ruthlessness, and they deserve to be printed out and pinned to the wall of every energy ministry on the planet. Not as humiliation. As inoculation.

## The Factory That Changed the Curve

Swanson's Law works because of learning curves and economies of scale, and no country in the history of solar energy has demonstrated both with more ferocity than China.

In 2001, China manufactured essentially no solar panels. By 2023, it manufactured roughly eighty per cent of the world's supply. The scale of this transformation is difficult to overstate. China now produces more solar panels in a single year than the entire world had cumulatively installed by 2012. The factories are enormous. The logistics are ruthless. The prices they have driven are the primary reason that the cost curve bent as sharply as it did.

This is not a simple story of cheap labour. The Chinese solar industry scaled through a combination of state-directed industrial policy, enormous capital investment, ferocious domestic competition that drove out weaker manufacturers, and genuine technical improvement in manufacturing processes. Polysilicon purification got cheaper. Wafers got thinner. Cell efficiency climbed from the mid-teens to above twenty-five per cent for commercial panels. Each improvement compounded on the others. The result was a manufacturing ecosystem so efficient that by the mid-2020s, Chinese solar panels were arriving at ports around the world at prices that manufacturers in other countries simply could not match, which created an entirely separate set of geopolitical complications that are beyond the scope of this article but are, it is fair to say, not uncomplicated.

## The Leapfrog

And here is the thing that really gets you. The cost collapse did not just make solar competitive in wealthy countries with established grids and supportive policy environments. It made solar viable in places that had never had reliable electricity at all.

If your phone battery had improved at the same rate as solar panel costs declined, you would charge it once a year and it would cost roughly the price of a stick of gum. The phone battery, of course, has done nothing of the sort, which is one of the quieter injustices of modern technology. But solar panels did, and the consequences in the developing world have been profound.

In sub-Saharan Africa, roughly six hundred million people lack access to reliable grid electricity. Building a conventional power grid to reach rural communities across a continent is eye-wateringly expensive and takes decades. But a solar panel, a battery, and a charge controller can be installed on a roof in an afternoon. The cost of a basic solar home system -- enough to power lights, charge a phone, and run a small radio -- has fallen to under fifty dollars in some markets. Organisations like Solar Aid have distributed millions of solar lights to replace kerosene lamps, which are expensive to run, dim, toxic to breathe, and occasionally set houses on fire. The replacement technology is cheaper, brighter, safer, and powered by an energy source that will not run out for five billion years. This is, by any reasonable measure, the kind of technological substitution that usually only happens in textbooks.

The pattern has a name: leapfrogging. Just as much of Africa skipped landline telephones and went straight to mobile phones, parts of the continent are skipping centralised grid infrastructure and going straight to distributed solar. Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia have seen explosive growth in pay-as-you-go solar systems, where families pay a small daily fee via mobile money -- the same M-Pesa system that reorganised Kenyan banking -- and receive electricity that is both cheaper and more reliable than the kerosene it replaces. The future of energy in much of the developing world may not look like the past of energy in the developed world, and the cost collapse is the reason.

## Perovskites and What Comes Next

The silicon solar cell that Bell Labs demonstrated in 1954 has been refined, optimised, and mass-manufactured to within an inch of its theoretical limits. Commercial panels now convert twenty-two to twenty-five per cent of incoming sunlight to electricity. The theoretical maximum for a single-junction silicon cell -- the Shockley-Queisser limit -- is about thirty-three per cent. There is room to improve, but the room is getting smaller.

Which is why a class of materials called perovskites has generated something close to genuine excitement among materials scientists, who are not, as a profession, given to excitability. Perovskite solar cells can be manufactured at low temperatures, potentially printed onto flexible substrates, and -- critically -- layered on top of existing silicon cells in what are called tandem configurations. A silicon-perovskite tandem cell can theoretically exceed the Shockley-Queisser limit because the two materials absorb different portions of the solar spectrum. Laboratory efficiencies above thirty-three per cent have already been demonstrated.

The catch, as of this writing, is durability. Silicon panels last twenty-five to thirty years in the field. Perovskite cells degrade faster, particularly when exposed to moisture and heat, which is a meaningful drawback for a technology whose entire purpose is to sit outside in the weather. But progress on stability has been rapid, and several manufacturers have announced plans for commercial tandem panels within the next few years. If perovskites work at scale, the cost curve may have another cliff face to fall off.

## What the Good News Does Not Tell You

A possiblist holds two things at once, and does not flinch.

The sun does not shine at night. This sentence is so obvious that writing it feels faintly embarrassing, but it is the central challenge of a solar-dominated energy system, and no amount of cost reduction in panels changes it. Intermittency is real. A grid powered primarily by solar energy requires either enormous amounts of storage, a complementary source of generation that can dispatch on demand, or both. Battery costs have also fallen dramatically -- roughly ninety per cent since 2010, in a pleasing parallel -- but grid-scale storage at the level required for a fully renewable grid remains expensive, technically challenging, and nowhere near deployed at sufficient scale.

The headline cost of solar -- four cents per kilowatt-hour -- is a levelized cost of energy, which means it averages the total cost of building and operating a solar farm over its lifetime. It does not include the cost of integrating that power into a grid, which involves transmission infrastructure, grid balancing, and backup generation for cloudy days and dark nights. These integration costs are not trivial. They vary enormously by geography and grid design. In some analyses, they roughly double the effective cost of solar electricity, which still leaves it competitive with fossil fuels in most places, but the gap is narrower than the headline number suggests.

Manufacturing solar panels is not, itself, a zero-emission activity. Producing polysilicon requires high temperatures and significant energy, much of which -- particularly in China -- comes from coal-fired power. A solar panel's carbon footprint is far lower than the fossil fuel electricity it displaces over its lifetime, but it is not zero, and pretending otherwise is the kind of thing that makes sceptics justifiably irritated. The lifecycle emissions of solar are roughly twenty to fifty grams of CO2 per kilowatt-hour, compared to roughly four hundred to a thousand for coal. The comparison is overwhelming, but the number is not nothing.

There is also the question of materials. Solar panels require silicon, silver, aluminium, and copper, all of which must be mined. Some thin-film technologies use cadmium and tellurium. The supply chains are concentrated. The environmental impacts of mining are real. End-of-life recycling infrastructure for solar panels is, in most countries, somewhere between nascent and nonexistent, which means that the first generation of panels installed at scale will, in twenty-five years, become several hundred million panels' worth of waste that somebody will need to deal with.

The last twenty per cent of decarbonisation may cost more than the first eighty per cent. The easy substitutions -- replacing coal plants with solar farms in sunny locations -- are happening. The hard ones -- reliable clean power at night, in winter, in cloudy northern latitudes, for industrial heat, for aviation, for shipping -- are a different order of problem. Solar's cost collapse is necessary for the energy transition. It is not sufficient.

## The Ratio

Here is what happened. A technology that was too expensive for anything except satellites in the 1960s became too expensive for anything except off-grid cabins in the 1990s, became competitive with retail electricity in the 2010s, and became the cheapest source of new electricity generation in human history by the 2020s. Every serious forecast underestimated the speed. Every expert projection was too conservative. The learning curve did not care what the models predicted.

The world now installs roughly three hundred gigawatts of new solar capacity per year, which is more new generating capacity than all other sources combined. If you had told an energy analyst in 2000 that this would happen by 2025, they would have been polite about it, in the way that professionals are polite when they think you are not serious.

There is something quietly remarkable about a species that spent millennia burning things for energy, looked up, noticed an enormous fusion reactor that had been running on a five-billion-year fuel supply and delivering energy to its doorstep for free the entire time, and said: "Right. Let us work out how to use that." It took us a while. The forecasters got it wrong. The curve was faster than anyone expected.

The sun is patient.
