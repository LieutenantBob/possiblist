Somewhere above your head, roughly fifteen to thirty-five kilometres up, there is a thin veil of gas that is the only reason anything on the surface of this planet is alive rather than irradiated. It is not thick. If you compressed the entire ozone layer down to sea-level pressure, it would be about three millimetres deep. The thickness of two stacked coins is all that stands between the biosphere and the kind of ultraviolet radiation that sterilises laboratory equipment. This is, if you pause to consider it, a somewhat precarious arrangement for a planet that has put four and a half billion years of work into developing multicellular life.

In the 1980s, we discovered we were destroying it. In the 1990s, we stopped. And then, in a feat of collective memory management that would impress any IT department tasked with archiving inconvenient files, we forgot the whole thing had happened.

When Possiblist's pilot survey asked people what had happened to the ozone hole since the Montreal Protocol banned CFCs, fifty-five per cent got it wrong. The most common incorrect answer was that it had stopped growing but had not shrunk -- a sort of atmospheric stalemate, as if the ozone layer and the chemicals eating it had negotiated a ceasefire but nobody had signed a peace deal. Others believed it was still getting worse. A meaningful number of respondents gave answers suggesting they had filed the ozone layer in the same mental cabinet as acid rain and Y2K: problems from the twentieth century whose resolution they could not quite recall and were too embarrassed to look up. I was, until I sat down with the data, firmly in this category. I could not have told you whether the ozone layer was recovering, stable, or still deteriorating, which is a remarkable thing to not know about what may be the single greatest act of international cooperation in the history of the species.

The ozone layer is recovering. It has been recovering for roughly two decades. Scientists expect it to return to its 1980 baseline by approximately 2066. A hole in the sky, caused by human industrial activity, identified by science, addressed by treaty, and now measurably healing on a timeline that will, for once, be met. If you wanted one piece of evidence that global cooperation on existential environmental threats is not merely theoretically possible but has actually been done, successfully, by the very civilisation currently struggling to agree on anything else, this is it.

The story of how it happened involves two chemists who were laughed at, a corporation that fought the science and then quietly switched sides, a treaty that nobody at a dinner party can name, and the extremely specific chemistry of what ultraviolet light does to a chlorine atom at minus seventy-eight degrees Celsius.

It is worth knowing. Most of it is behind the door.

## Two Chemists Walk Into an Atmosphere

In 1974, Frank Sherwood Rowland and Mario Molina published a paper in *Nature* that, in retrospect, contained one of the most consequential sentences in the history of environmental science. They proposed that chlorofluorocarbons -- CFCs, the chemicals used in refrigerators, air conditioners, aerosol sprays, and the general machinery of mid-century convenience -- were drifting up into the stratosphere and destroying ozone molecules. The mechanism was elegant and horrifying: a single chlorine atom, liberated from a CFC molecule by ultraviolet light, could catalytically destroy tens of thousands of ozone molecules before finally being deactivated. One atom. Tens of thousands. The chemistry was not complicated. The implications were.

The response of the scientific establishment was, initially, the response of any establishment confronted with news it would prefer not to be true. Rowland recalled being treated as something between a crank and a saboteur. The aerosol industry called the hypothesis "a science fiction tale." The chairman of DuPont -- which manufactured about a quarter of the world's CFCs -- declared that the ozone depletion theory was "a science fiction tale, a load of rubbish, utter nonsense." DuPont spent years funding counter-research and lobbying against regulation, which is the kind of behaviour that is always described as "vigorous defence of shareholder interests" in the annual report and "knowingly delaying action on an existential threat" everywhere else.

Then, in 1985, Joe Farman and his colleagues at the British Antarctic Survey published measurements showing that the ozone layer over Antarctica had thinned by roughly forty per cent since the late 1970s. They had, in fact, been sitting on anomalous readings for several years because the numbers were so extreme they assumed their instruments were broken. They replaced the instruments. The numbers remained extreme. When NASA went back and checked its own satellite data -- data that had been automatically flagged as erroneous and discarded by the processing software -- the hole was there too. The most sophisticated atmospheric monitoring system on Earth had detected the problem and then programmed itself to ignore it, which is either a cautionary tale about automation or a metaphor for how the human brain handles uncomfortable information. Possibly both.

The ozone hole was not subtle. It was an area of severe depletion the size of North America, appearing every Antarctic spring with the regularity of a particularly unwelcome season. The images were visceral. For perhaps the first time, an invisible atmospheric process had a shape that could be printed in a newspaper and understood by anyone who had ever seen a bruise.

## The Treaty Nobody Can Name

In September 1987 -- two years after the hole was confirmed, thirteen years after Rowland and Molina's paper -- the nations of the world signed the Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer. It required the phaseout of CFCs and other ozone-depleting substances. It has since been ratified by every country on Earth. Every single one. There is no other treaty in human history that can make this claim. The United Nations Charter itself has not been universally ratified. The agreement to stop destroying the ozone layer has broader support than the agreement to have a United Nations.

If you mentioned the Montreal Protocol at a dinner party, most people would assume you were talking about a Jason Bourne film. This is the most successful international environmental agreement ever signed, and it has the public profile of a regional zoning regulation.

What made it work? Several things, and they are instructive precisely because they illuminate why other problems have proven harder.

First, the science was unambiguous early. Rowland and Molina's chemistry was clear. The Antarctic hole was measurable. There was no equivalent of the climate debate's long tail of "yes, but natural variability" -- the hole was there, it was new, and it was caused by chemicals whose atmospheric pathways were well understood. When the evidence is a continent-sized wound in the sky that was not there twenty years ago, the space for motivated scepticism narrows considerably.

Second, the affected industries were concentrated. CFC production was dominated by a relatively small number of companies in a relatively small number of countries. DuPont alone accounted for about twenty-five per cent of global production. When DuPont's own scientists confirmed the ozone depletion mechanism in the mid-1980s, the company executed one of the more remarkable pivots in corporate history: it dropped its opposition, announced it would phase out CFC production, and began developing alternatives. The cynical reading is that DuPont saw the regulatory writing on the wall and moved to capture the replacement market. The generous reading is that the science became undeniable and a major corporation did the right thing. Both readings are probably true simultaneously, which is how most progress actually works. The moral purity of the motivation matters less than the atmospheric chemistry of the outcome.

Third, substitutes existed. Hydrochlorofluorocarbons and later hydrofluorocarbons could do most of what CFCs did. The transition was not painless -- it cost the refrigeration industry billions -- but it was not existential. Nobody had to give up air conditioning. Nobody had to restructure the global energy system. The ask was: use a different chemical in the same machine. Compare this to climate change, where the ask is: restructure the energy infrastructure of every economy on Earth. The scale of substitution is not the same, and pretending otherwise does a disservice to both problems.

## The Long, Slow Mend

The ozone layer's recovery is real, measurable, and agonisingly slow. This is because CFCs are extraordinarily persistent. A CFC molecule released from an aerosol can in 1985 is, in all likelihood, still in the atmosphere. The average atmospheric lifetime of CFC-12, the most common variety, is approximately one hundred years. The chemicals are still up there, still catalysing the destruction of ozone molecules, just in declining concentrations as the sources were shut off decades ago.

The numbers tell the story with the particular eloquence of things that move in the right direction at a pace that tests human patience. Antarctic ozone readings have climbed from their nadir of roughly 100 Dobson units in the mid-1990s to approximately 180 Dobson units in 2024. The pre-depletion baseline was about 280. The trajectory is clear. The timeline is not short. Full recovery of the Antarctic ozone layer is expected around 2066. The Arctic, which suffers less severe but still significant depletion, should recover by the 2040s. The mid-latitudes -- where most humans live -- are expected to return to 1980 levels by roughly 2040.

And here is the thing that really gets you. This is a deadline humanity will actually meet. Not because of heroic last-minute effort or emergency summits or dramatic intervention, but because the treaty worked, the chemistry is predictable, and the banned substances are slowly, molecule by molecule, breaking down in the upper atmosphere. The ozone layer will be whole again within the lifetimes of children alive today. It will happen not with a flourish but with the quiet, cumulative arithmetic of atmospheric chemistry doing what atmospheric chemistry does when you stop poisoning it.

The Montreal Protocol has also proved adaptable in a way that few international agreements manage. The Kigali Amendment, adopted in 2016, extended the treaty's reach to hydrofluorocarbons -- the very chemicals that replaced CFCs. HFCs do not damage the ozone layer, but they are potent greenhouse gases, some with a warming potential thousands of times greater than carbon dioxide. The amendment aims to reduce HFC consumption by eighty per cent by the late 2040s and could avoid up to 0.5 degrees Celsius of global warming by 2100. A treaty designed to fix one atmospheric problem has been repurposed to help with another. This is the policy equivalent of discovering that the spare tyre also works as a flotation device.

## The Complications, Because There Are Always Complications

A possiblist holds the complication alongside the progress, and does not look away from either.

Recovery is not uniform. The polar vortex -- the ring of cold air that isolates the Antarctic stratosphere each winter -- creates conditions uniquely hostile to ozone. At temperatures below minus seventy-eight degrees Celsius, polar stratospheric clouds form, and their surfaces catalyse the chemical reactions that convert stable chlorine compounds into ozone-destroying radicals. As long as CFCs persist in the atmosphere, every Antarctic winter will produce some degree of ozone depletion. The hole will shrink, but it will not simply close like a wound healing from the edges inward. It will fluctuate, some years worse than others, driven by stratospheric temperatures and volcanic aerosols and the chaotic dynamics of polar meteorology, until the chlorine loading finally drops below the threshold where the vortex chemistry matters.

In 2018, researchers detected a mysterious surge in CFC-11 emissions that should not have existed. The Montreal Protocol had banned CFC-11 production years earlier. Atmospheric measurements were unambiguous: somebody, somewhere, was making the stuff. Investigation traced the emissions primarily to eastern China, where rogue manufacturers were using CFC-11 as a cheap foam-blowing agent. The emissions have since declined sharply -- following international pressure and Chinese government enforcement -- but the episode demonstrated that even the most successful treaty in history requires ongoing vigilance. Compliance is not a state. It is a practice.

There is also the uncomfortable question of transferability. The Montreal Protocol worked because the problem had features that made it solvable: identifiable chemicals, concentrated producers, available substitutes, unambiguous science, and a threat vivid enough to generate political will. Climate change has unambiguous science but diffuse sources, no simple substitutes, and a threat that manifests slowly enough for each generation to believe it is someone else's problem. The ozone story is proof that global cooperation on atmospheric threats is possible. It is not proof that global cooperation on atmospheric threats is easy, or that the template transfers directly to a problem wired into the energy system of every economy on Earth.

## A Quiet Closing

Sherwood Rowland, near the end of his life, was asked what had surprised him most about the ozone story. He said it was not the science. The science had been straightforward. What surprised him was how long it took for anyone to act on it. Eleven years from the 1974 paper to the discovery of the hole. Thirteen years to the Montreal Protocol. The science was there from the beginning. What was missing was the willingness to find it inconvenient.

The ozone layer will fully recover within forty years. It will do so without fanfare, without a ceremony, without a headline. The atmosphere will simply return to the state it was in before we accidentally punched a hole in it, and most people alive at the time will have no idea it happened, just as most people alive now have no idea it is happening.

A species identified a threat to the global commons, negotiated a binding international agreement, implemented it, and watched the problem begin to reverse. Then it forgot. Filed the whole thing away somewhere between the fall of the Berlin Wall and the invention of the DVD. Moved on.

There is a word for the kind of person who finds this both exasperating and encouraging. Who holds the forgetting and the fixing in the same hand and does not drop either one. Who looks at the data and sees not a reason for complacency, nor a reason for despair, but evidence -- actual, measurable, atmospheric evidence -- that the hardest things are possible.

The ozone layer is healing. Quietly, and on schedule.
