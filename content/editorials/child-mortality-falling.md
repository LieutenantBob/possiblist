In 1990, approximately twelve point six million children died before reaching their fifth birthday. That is a number so large that the human mind does not really process it — it files it alongside "a lot" and moves on. This is understandable. The mind was not designed for numbers like these.

By 2024, that number had fallen to under five million. This means that every year, compared to 1990 rates, roughly seven and a half million children survive who would not have done. To put that in terms the mind can actually hold: that is the population of a mid-sized European country, saved annually, through a combination of interventions so unglamorous they could barely make it into a funding brochure.

The decline was driven by no single breakthrough. It was oral rehydration therapy — literally a packet of salt, sugar, and water — for diarrhoeal diseases. It was expanded vaccination coverage. It was better nutrition. It was mosquito nets. It was the quiet, relentless, undramatic work of community health workers in places that do not make the evening news.

Sixty-eight per cent of people do not know this has happened. They believe child mortality has either stayed the same or gotten worse. This is not because they are not paying attention. It is because good news at this scale arrives one healthy child at a time, in places that cameras do not visit, and is therefore functionally invisible to the news cycle.

The story of oral rehydration therapy deserves its own moment, because it contains within it a microcosm of how the world actually gets better.

In the late 1960s, a Bangladeshi doctor named Dilip Mahalanabis faced a cholera epidemic in refugee camps during the Bangladesh Liberation War. The standard treatment — intravenous saline drip — required equipment, training, and sterile conditions that simply did not exist. Mahalanabis had a mixture of salt, sugar, and water. He had some evidence it might work. He had thousands of dying children. He used it.

It worked. The death rate from cholera in his camps dropped from thirty per cent to under four per cent. The treatment cost roughly two pence per dose. It has since been estimated that oral rehydration therapy has saved over fifty million lives worldwide — making it, by any reasonable measure, one of the most cost-effective medical interventions in human history. The Lancet called it "potentially the most important medical advance of the twentieth century." It was a packet of salt and sugar.

This is the pattern that repeats throughout the child mortality story: unglamorous interventions, delivered at scale, producing results that are individually invisible and collectively staggering. Vaccination campaigns did not make the front page. Improved water treatment in small towns across South Asia did not trend on social media. The slow expansion of antenatal care across sub-Saharan Africa was not the subject of a documentary.

But the children lived. That is the point. Seven and a half million more of them per year than in 1990. And yet the world, when asked, believes things have stayed the same or gotten worse.

There is, in this gap between reality and perception, something that should give us pause. Not despair — despair would be precisely the wrong response to one of the greatest achievements in human history. But pause.

If the most cost-effective medical intervention of the twentieth century was a packet of salt and sugar, and it took decades for most people to learn it existed — what other mundane, unglamorous, life-saving things might be working right now, in places we are not looking?
