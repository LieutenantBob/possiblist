The universe is approximately thirteen point eight billion years old, and for almost all of that time it contained no children. This was not a policy decision. It was simply that the universe spent most of its existence producing hydrogen, helium, and the occasional supernova, none of which require nappies or school uniforms or an opinion about screen time. Then, roughly three hundred thousand years ago, one particular species of primate arrived on one particular rocky planet and began having children at a rate that suggested it rather enjoyed the process, and immediately encountered a problem that would persist for the next two hundred and ninety-nine thousand nine hundred and sixty-odd years: the children kept dying.

They died of things that had names and things that did not. They died of fevers, of flux, of bad water, of no water, of infections so commonplace that for most of recorded history a parent burying a child was not tragedy but arithmetic. In medieval England, parish records suggest that roughly one in three children died before the age of five. In some periods it was closer to one in two. The species had developed language, agriculture, architecture, philosophy, mathematics, several incompatible systems of government, oil painting, counterpoint, and the ability to build cathedrals that took longer to construct than most of its children lived, and it had not yet worked out how to reliably keep them alive past the age when they could hold a spoon. This was, if you were a child, suboptimal. By 1800, the global under-five mortality rate was still roughly forty-three per cent. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries would bring improvements in sanitation, nutrition, and medicine that bent the curve dramatically in wealthy nations. But as recently as 1990 -- which is to say, within the living memory of anyone reading this who is old enough to remember a world without Google -- ninety-three out of every thousand children born on Earth still did not make it to their fifth birthday.

When Gapminder posed the question to audiences across fourteen countries -- has child mortality improved, worsened, or stayed the same since 1990? -- sixty-eight per cent got it wrong. I was among them, or would have been, which is the same thing for the purposes of honesty. Most people guessed it had stayed roughly the same. Some believed it had gotten worse. A chimpanzee selecting answers at random would have outperformed the human average, which is becoming a recurring theme in these articles and is not, it must be said, a theme that reflects especially well on the species. The pattern is now familiar enough to be unsettling: on question after question about whether the world is getting better or worse, the average human performs with a consistency that can only be explained by the existence of systematic wrongness. Hans Rosling tested this question on Nobel laureates in Sweden. He tested it on senior staff at the World Bank. He tested it on delegates at Davos, the annual gathering of the people who are supposed to know how the world works. They all scored worse than random chance, which means they would have done better putting down their programmes, closing their eyes, and throwing a dart. We are not guessing randomly. We are guessing wrong, with commitment.

The truth is that the under-five mortality rate has fallen from ninety-three per thousand in 1990 to approximately thirty-six per thousand in 2024. In absolute numbers, annual child deaths dropped from twelve point six million to under five million. That means roughly seven and a half million additional children survive each year compared to 1990 rates. Every year. To put that in terms the mind can actually hold: imagine the entire population of Portugal -- every person in every city from Lisbon to Porto and all the villages in between -- and then imagine saving all of them. That is what happens annually, through interventions so mundane they could not get themselves invited to a TED Talk. No single breakthrough. No eureka moment. No charismatic billionaire descended from a helicopter to announce it. Just salt, sugar, water, vaccines, mosquito nets, and an army of community health workers in places the cameras never visit, doing the same unglamorous thing over and over until the numbers bent.

A newspaper could have run the headline "TWENTY THOUSAND FEWER CHILDREN DIED TODAY THAN ON THIS DATE IN 1990" every single day for thirty-four years and it would have been approximately true on every one of those days. It was never a headline. And the most important of those interventions -- the one The Lancet called "potentially the most important medical advance of the twentieth century" -- turned out to be a solution you could mix in a kitchen with ingredients that cost less than a postage stamp.

## The Solution That Was Too Simple to Believe

The history of medicine is full of moments where a cure was rejected not because it did not work but because it could not possibly be that easy. Oral rehydration therapy is perhaps the purest example.

Diarrhoeal disease was, for most of human history, one of the great killers of children. It still is, in places, but in 1980 it killed approximately four point six million children per year. The mechanism is grimly straightforward: the child loses fluid faster than the body can replace it, and dies of dehydration. The treatment that existed was intravenous saline -- effective, but requiring sterile equipment, trained medical staff, a reliable power supply, and a hospital. In the villages of Bangladesh in 1971, during the Liberation War, with millions of refugees crammed into camps and cholera tearing through them, none of these things existed.

What did exist was a Bangladeshi physician named Dilip Mahalanabis, an epidemic, and a radical idea that the medical establishment had been arguing about for a decade. The science was not new. Researchers had known since the early 1960s that the gut could absorb water if glucose was present, even during severe diarrhoea -- a discovery that the physiologist Robert Crane's work on sodium-glucose co-transport had made possible in 1960. What nobody had done was trust this knowledge with actual dying children outside a research ward.

Mahalanabis did. In the refugee camps near Calcutta, with cholera patients arriving faster than they could be connected to IV drips, he mixed salt, sugar, and water in buckets and had volunteers -- not doctors, not nurses, volunteers -- administer it by mouth. The death rate in his camps dropped from roughly thirty per cent to three point six per cent. He published the results. The medical community responded with the enthusiasm typically reserved for a colleague who has skipped several important steps in the peer review process, which, to be fair, he had.

It took years. The World Health Organization did not officially endorse oral rehydration therapy until 1978. UNICEF, under the leadership of James Grant, who had the useful quality of being both relentless and uninterested in the word "no," made it the centrepiece of a campaign called GOBI -- an acronym for Growth monitoring, Oral rehydration, Breastfeeding, and Immunisation. Grant's strategy was disarmingly simple: go to every country, talk to every head of state, and refuse to leave until they agreed to distribute packets of oral rehydration salts. He carried the packets in his jacket pocket. He pulled them out at state dinners. He was, by several accounts, extremely tiring to sit next to, and he saved millions of lives.

The cost of a single dose of oral rehydration salts is approximately ten cents. The treatment requires no electricity, no medical degree, and no equipment beyond a container and clean water. It can be mixed by a parent, administered by a parent, and has a success rate in treating mild to moderate dehydration that rivals interventions costing thousands of times more. It has been estimated that oral rehydration therapy has saved over fifty million lives since its widespread adoption. Fifty million. If those people formed a country, it would be the thirtieth largest on Earth, somewhere between South Korea and Colombia, populated entirely by individuals who survived because someone mixed the right ratio of salt and sugar into water and handed it to them in a cup.

The cure for one of the great killers of children turned out to be a recipe. If a pharmaceutical company had invented it, it would have cost four hundred dollars a dose and had a three-syllable brand name. Instead it costs pennies and saves millions of lives per year, which is precisely the kind of thing that does not get a Super Bowl advert. That it took the global medical establishment nearly two decades to fully accept this says something about the relationship between simplicity and credibility, a relationship that remains, to put it gently, complicated.

## The Campaign That Nobody Filmed

James Grant's GOBI strategy was one component of what became the largest coordinated public health effort in history, and it is worth pausing to note that almost none of it was televised.

The Expanded Programme on Immunisation, launched by the WHO in 1974, set out to vaccinate every child on Earth against six diseases: tuberculosis, diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, polio, and measles. In 1974, roughly five per cent of the world's children were vaccinated. By 1990, coverage had reached approximately eighty per cent. The logistics of this -- getting temperature-sensitive vaccines to villages without roads, without refrigeration, without reliable maps -- were solved not by some grand technological breakthrough but by cold chains made of insulated boxes and motorbikes, and by health workers who walked. Measles alone killed roughly 2.6 million children per year in the 1980s. By 2019, that number had fallen to just over two hundred thousand. The measles vaccine costs less than a dollar per dose. The arithmetic of this -- $1, one injection, one child who does not die of measles -- is so straightforward that it makes you wonder why arithmetic does not feature more prominently in political campaigns.

Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, founded in 2000 with a seven hundred and fifty million dollar grant from the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation, has since helped vaccinate over one billion children in the world's poorest countries. One billion is a number that the human mind processes in much the same way it processes "a lot" -- by nodding vaguely and moving on. But consider: Gavi estimates that its programmes have prevented over seventeen million deaths. The organisation operates with all the charisma of a logistics company, because that is essentially what it is. It negotiates bulk vaccine prices, coordinates cold chain infrastructure, and ensures that small glass vials arrive intact in places where the nearest paved road is a three-day walk. It is, in every meaningful sense, one of the most effective organisations in human history, and it has the public profile of a mid-tier accounting firm.

In Bangladesh, BRAC -- the organisation founded by Fazle Hasan Abed after the Liberation War -- trained women from villages to go door to door teaching mothers how to mix oral rehydration solution at home. Not with the official WHO packets, which were not available in sufficient quantities, but with a pinch of salt and a fistful of sugar in a specific ratio, mixed in water. They called it "lobon-gur" solution: salt and molasses. BRAC trained over twelve million families. Twelve million. One woman at a time, standing in a doorway, demonstrating the pinch. Diarrhoeal deaths in Bangladesh fell by more than eighty per cent over the following decades. The intervention was a recipe and a conversation. It is not the sort of thing that wins design awards.

Ethiopia, a country whose name in the Western imagination remains tethered to the 1984 famine despite everything that has happened since, reduced its under-five mortality rate from two hundred and five per thousand in 1990 to roughly fifty per thousand by 2019. The mechanism was a Health Extension Programme that trained and deployed over thirty-eight thousand community health workers -- nearly all women -- into every rural kebele in the country. They delivered vaccines, taught sanitation, distributed mosquito nets, and tracked pregnancies. The programme cost approximately seven dollars per person per year. Seven dollars. The price of a mediocre sandwich in any airport where you might read about Ethiopia's problems in a complimentary newspaper.

Rwanda's trajectory is, if anything, more striking. After the 1994 genocide, the country's health infrastructure was, to use the technical term, destroyed. Under-five mortality stood at two hundred and thirty per thousand in the mid-1990s -- nearly one in four children. By 2020, it had fallen to forty-five per thousand. The Rwandan government introduced community-based health insurance covering over ninety per cent of the population, trained forty-five thousand community health workers, and built a system in which every village had someone whose job it was to know which children needed vaccinations and which pregnant women needed iron supplements. Drones now deliver blood to remote clinics. The distance from genocide to drone-delivered blood supplies is twenty-six years, which is a sentence that contains more historical information than most books.

## The Geography of What Remains

And here is where a possiblist earns the name.

The global average -- thirty-six per thousand -- is an average in the way that a person with one hand in a fire and one hand in a freezer has, on average, a comfortable temperature. Sub-Saharan Africa still loses children at rates above seventy per thousand in several countries. Somalia, Chad, and the Central African Republic remain above one hundred per thousand, which means that roughly one in ten children dies before their fifth birthday. In Finland, the rate is two per thousand. A child born in Chad is approximately fifty times more likely to die before age five than a child born in Helsinki. They are born on the same planet, in the same year, into the same species. The postcode does the rest.

The rate of decline has also slowed. Between 1990 and 2015, under-five mortality fell at an average annual rate of three point four per cent. Since 2015, the rate has dropped to two point three per cent. The Sustainable Development Goal -- reducing under-five mortality to twenty-five per thousand everywhere by 2030 -- will not be met. Not approximately not met. Definitively, mathematically, unless something changes dramatically, not met.

COVID-19 disrupted vaccination campaigns, diverted health workers, and closed clinics across the developing world. UNICEF estimated that twenty-three million children missed routine vaccinations in 2020 alone -- the highest number in over a decade. Measles, which had been retreating steadily for thirty years, surged. The setback was not permanent, but it was real, and it fell, as these things invariably do, on the children who could least afford it.

There is also the neonatal problem, which is in some ways the shadow cast by success. Deaths in the first twenty-eight days of life now account for nearly half of all under-five deaths, up from roughly a third in 1990. This is not because neonatal deaths have increased -- they have fallen too, from five million per year to roughly two point three million. It is because deaths after the neonatal period have fallen so much faster. The success of vaccines and oral rehydration and mosquito nets pushed the later deaths down dramatically, and what emerged from beneath them was the stubborn floor of complications at birth: prematurity, birth asphyxia, neonatal sepsis. These are not problems that yield to a packet of salt and sugar. They require skilled birth attendants, functioning delivery rooms, incubators, and the kind of medical infrastructure that takes decades and serious money to build.

The 2003 Lancet child survival series -- a landmark set of papers that essentially mapped the terrain of what was killing children and what could stop it -- identified a set of interventions that, if delivered at scale, could prevent roughly two thirds of all under-five deaths. Most were remarkably simple and cheap. But the neonatal interventions were harder: kangaroo mother care for premature babies, clean cord care, neonatal resuscitation. These are not technologies. They are skills, and skills require trained people, and trained people require functioning systems that pay them and supply them and do not collapse during the next political crisis. The easy wins -- and calling them "easy" is itself a kind of insult to the millions of health workers who delivered them -- have been won. What remains is harder.

## The Arithmetic of Attention

The most important thing happening on any given Tuesday in the 1990s and 2000s was the slow, incremental, unfilmable survival of children in places whose names most readers could not pronounce, through interventions whose mechanisms most readers would find boring, administered by people whose names will never appear in any newspaper for any reason. It happened on Wednesdays too. And Thursdays. For thirty-four consecutive years.

This is not a failure of journalism. It is a structural feature of how attention works. Johan Galtung and Mari Holmboe Ruge identified it in 1965: events are newsworthy if they are sudden, negative, and involve powerful nations. Slow, positive, structural change in poor countries scores zero on every criterion. The greatest rescue operation in the history of the species is, by the formal definition of news values, not news. It does not even qualify as a slow news day, because on slow news days editors run stories about unusual weather and animals that resemble celebrities, not stories about the systematic prevention of childhood death in equatorial Africa.

Steven Pinker has observed that "things are complicated and getting slowly better in most ways while getting worse in some ways" is a sentence that has never gone viral. There is a reason. Our brains evolved to notice threats, not trends. The rustle in the bushes might be a predator. The gradual disappearance of childhood death, however welcome, has never eaten anyone. We are, as a species, magnificently equipped to detect danger and spectacularly ill-equipped to notice improvement, which is an unfortunate combination of traits for an animal that has recently gotten rather good at improving things.

Max Roser of Our World in Data has noted that a newspaper could have run the headline "A COUNTRY THAT MIGHT HAVE HAD A FAMINE DID NOT HAVE ONE TODAY" every single day for decades, and it would have been accurate. It would also have been terrible journalism. The absence of catastrophe is, by definition, invisible. A child who does not die of diarrhoea does not make a sound. A village where measles does not arrive does not hold a press conference. The most successful public health interventions in history are defined by the things that do not happen, and things that do not happen are, informationally speaking, silent. This is a feature of how reality works, not a bug. It is just a feature that happens to leave several billion people with a wildly inaccurate picture of the planet they live on.

And yet the children survived. Seven and a half million more per year than in 1990, through salt and sugar and water, through vaccines that cost less than a cup of coffee, through women walking to doorways in villages that do not appear on most maps, through the accumulated, unspectacular, life-saving tedium of checking which children have been vaccinated and which have not.

There is a question that sits at the centre of this, and it does not have a comfortable answer. If slightly salty sugar water can save fifty million lives, and the world took twenty years to fully accept this -- if a seven-dollar-per-person health programme can halve child mortality in Ethiopia, and most people have never heard of it -- what else is working, right now, in the places we are not looking?

A possiblist does not answer that question. A possiblist notices it.

The children lived. That is the fact with weight.
