The species has a talent for producing things it then fails to distribute. This is true of wealth, of medical knowledge, of sensible advice about flossing, and -- perhaps most conspicuously -- of food. We grow enough of it to feed approximately ten billion people, which is convenient in some respects and deeply inconvenient in others, given that only eight billion of us currently exist and roughly seven hundred million of those go to bed hungry on any given night. The surplus, in case you were wondering, does not sit politely in a warehouse awaiting instructions. About a third of all food produced on Earth is wasted -- lost in fields, spoiled in transit, forgotten at the back of refrigerators in countries wealthy enough to have refrigerators. The amount we throw away each year would be sufficient to feed every hungry person on the planet. Twice over. This is the kind of statistic that sounds like it must be wrong, and is not.

When Possiblist asked people what had happened to global hunger since the year 2000, fifty-nine per cent got it wrong. The most popular answer was that nothing much had changed -- that the problem was too big, too entrenched, too structural to have budged in any meaningful direction. This is the pessimism that dresses itself up as realism and goes unquestioned at dinner parties. I would have said something similar, not long ago. The gut feeling is that hunger is one of those permanent conditions of the species, like war, or the inability to assemble flat-pack furniture without one leftover screw.

The gut, in this case, is unreliable. The share of the world's population classified as undernourished has fallen from roughly fifteen per cent in 2000 to approximately nine per cent today. One in seven became one in eleven. Stated differently: in a quarter of a century, the world got about forty per cent better at feeding itself, and the most common response to this information is a polite shrug of disbelief. If you took every person who crossed out of chronic hunger since the millennium and lined them up, you would have a queue longer than any that has ever existed for any purpose, including the ones at Heathrow Terminal 5 during a systems outage. The queue would, in fact, contain more people than the entire population of South America.

The story of how this happened involves a man almost nobody has heard of, a grain almost everyone has eaten, a controversy about the colour of rice, and the largest school lunch programme in the history of the species. It also involves the part where progress stalled, reversed, and became considerably more complicated than the headline suggests.

That part matters too.

## The Man Who Fed a Billion People (Continued)

If you have read the Possiblist piece on extreme poverty, you will have encountered Norman Borlaug -- the agronomist who saved approximately one billion lives and has the name recognition of a regional insurance adjustor. That article told the story of his dwarf wheat and its role in averting famine in India and Pakistan. What it did not tell you -- because it was busy with poverty lines and purchasing-power parity -- is what happened next.

The Green Revolution that Borlaug set in motion was not a single event but a cascade. The high-yield wheat varieties developed in Mexico were adapted for South Asia in the 1960s. Then came the rice. The International Rice Research Institute in the Philippines produced IR8, a semi-dwarf rice variety that doubled yields under the right conditions. Farmers in the Philippines called it "miracle rice," which is the sort of name that would get you laughed out of a marketing department and which turned out to be approximately accurate. Between 1960 and 2000, global cereal production doubled while the amount of land under cultivation grew by only fifteen per cent. The arithmetic is worth pausing on. We fed twice as many mouths from roughly the same acreage. The remaining mouths were fed by ingenuity, not by conquest.

Borlaug himself was under no illusions. He called the Green Revolution a temporary success -- a way to buy time, not a permanent solution. "We are dealing with two opposing forces," he said in his Nobel lecture, "the scientific power of food production and the biologic power of human reproduction." He was seventy-six when he said that, and he had been right about nearly everything for thirty years, which is the kind of track record that ought to make people listen and almost never does.

The critique of the Green Revolution is real and should be stated plainly. It favoured large farms over small ones. It increased dependency on fertilisers and pesticides. It concentrated agricultural power in ways that left subsistence farmers more vulnerable, not less. In Punjab, the poster region for Green Revolution wheat, the water table has dropped so dramatically that farmers now drill bore wells deeper than some oil rigs. The revolution fed a billion people and left the soil exhausted. Both things are true. A possiblist holds them both.

## Hunger Is Not Famine

There is a distinction that matters enormously and that most people collapse into a single image. Famine is the thing on the news: acute, visible, catastrophic, usually driven by war or natural disaster, and affecting a specific population over a bounded period. Hunger -- chronic undernourishment -- is something else entirely. It is quieter. It kills more slowly. It does not photograph well. A child who is chronically undernourished does not look like the images in charity adverts; she looks like a slightly smaller version of her peers, and the damage is happening inside -- to brain development, to immune function, to the quiet architecture of a life that will be shorter and harder than it needed to be.

The distinction matters because the world has become extraordinarily good at responding to famine and frustratingly mediocre at addressing chronic hunger. When the cameras arrive, so does the aid. When the cameras leave, so does the attention. The seven hundred million people who are chronically undernourished are not, for the most part, in active famines. They are in the grey space between eating enough and eating enough of the right things, and that grey space does not trend on social media.

## The India Paradox

India is a nuclear power with a space programme and a tech sector that employs some of the most skilled engineers on Earth. India also has the highest rate of childhood stunting of any major economy. Roughly thirty-five per cent of Indian children under five are too short for their age -- not because of genetics, but because of chronic malnutrition during the first thousand days of life. The country that sends satellites to Mars has not yet worked out how to ensure that a third of its children grow to their full height. This is not a failure of resources. India produces more than enough food to feed its population. It is a failure of distribution, of sanitation, of the thousand small systems that connect a grain of rice in a warehouse to a child in a village in Jharkhand.

The Indian government's response has been, characteristically, enormous. The Mid-Day Meal Scheme feeds approximately 120 million children every school day, making it the largest school lunch programme in the history of the world. It was not designed as a nutrition programme. It was designed to get children into school -- the meal is the bribe, the education is the point. But the side effects turned out to be remarkable. Studies show that the programme increased school enrolment, reduced the gender gap in attendance (parents who would not send a daughter to school will send a daughter to lunch), and improved nutritional outcomes in the most food-insecure states. A programme designed to solve one problem quietly began solving three others. This happens more often than planners expect and less often than they claim.

## The School Lunch Revolution

India is not alone. Brazil's Programa Nacional de Alimentacao Escolar feeds forty million students daily, with a legal requirement that thirty per cent of ingredients be purchased from local family farms. The elegance of this is worth noting: the programme feeds children and creates a guaranteed market for small-scale agriculture simultaneously. It is a school lunch that is also an agricultural policy that is also a poverty intervention, which is the kind of efficient triple-duty that makes policy wonks involuntarily smile.

Across the developing world, school feeding programmes now reach approximately 418 million children in virtually every country on Earth. The World Food Programme calls it "the world's largest safety net." The evidence base is, by the standards of development economics, unusually robust: school meals increase attendance, improve cognitive performance, reduce anaemia in girls, and -- in a finding that should surprise nobody who has ever tried to concentrate while hungry -- improve test scores. The cost is between twenty-five and fifty cents per meal per child. A coin. Two coins. The distance between a child who can concentrate and a child who cannot is measured in the small change you would not bend down to retrieve from the pavement.

## The Golden Rice Wars

In the early 2000s, a group of scientists developed Golden Rice -- a genetically modified variety engineered to produce beta-carotene, which the human body converts to vitamin A. Vitamin A deficiency kills roughly 250,000 children per year and blinds twice that number. Golden Rice was designed to address this, and it was offered free to farmers earning less than $10,000 per year.

What followed was one of the more instructive episodes in the history of good intentions colliding with institutional inertia. Greenpeace opposed it. Anti-GMO activists destroyed test fields in the Philippines. Regulatory approval took over twenty years. The rice sat in laboratories while the children it was designed to help continued going blind at a rate of roughly 1,400 per day. In 2013, a group of 150 Nobel laureates signed a letter asking Greenpeace to cease its opposition, noting that "how many poor people in the world must die before we consider this a crime against humanity?" Greenpeace did not change its position. The Philippines finally approved Golden Rice for commercial cultivation in 2021. Bangladesh followed. The twenty-year delay, during which an estimated five million children died of vitamin A deficiency, is the kind of fact that resists tidy categorisation as either a cautionary tale about corporate science or a cautionary tale about activist overreach. It is, uncomfortably, both.

## The Reversal

And now the part that a responsible publication cannot omit, and that an irresponsible one would bury.

Progress stalled around 2015. The share of undernourished people, which had been falling steadily for fifteen years, flattened and then -- during COVID-19 -- rose. The pandemic disrupted supply chains, collapsed informal economies, and pushed an estimated 150 million additional people into food insecurity between 2019 and 2022. The recovery has been incomplete.

But COVID was an accelerant, not a cause. The underlying drivers of the stall are older and more structural. Climate change is introducing volatility into food systems that had been gradually stabilising. Droughts in the Horn of Africa, floods in Pakistan, heat waves in South Asia -- each event individually manageable, collectively destabilising. The farmers most affected are the ones who contributed least to the emissions causing the problem. The cruel arithmetic, once again.

And then there is conflict. Yemen's civil war created what the UN called the world's worst humanitarian crisis -- twenty-one million people in need of food assistance in a country of thirty million. South Sudan's civil war displaced four million people and left seven million food insecure. In these contexts, hunger is not a development problem. It is a weapon. Warring parties block aid convoys, destroy crops, and besiege civilian populations. The Geneva Conventions technically prohibit the use of starvation as a method of warfare. The word "technically" is doing rather a lot of work in that sentence.

The Sustainable Development Goal of zero hunger by 2030 is now, by quiet consensus among the people whose job it is to track these things, unachievable. Nobody has formally announced this. The goal sits on the UN website like a light left on in a room everyone has left. The number of undernourished people in sub-Saharan Africa has actually increased since 2015, driven by population growth that outpaces agricultural improvement -- the same pattern that complicates the poverty story, playing out again in a different register.

## The Arithmetic of Waste

Here is the fact that, once encountered, becomes difficult to think around. The world produces enough food to feed approximately ten billion people. Eight billion exist. Seven hundred million are hungry. One third of all food produced is lost or wasted. The tonnage wasted annually -- roughly 1.3 billion tonnes -- would be sufficient to feed every undernourished person on the planet not once but twice over.

The waste happens differently in different places. In wealthy countries, it happens at the consumer end: food purchased, forgotten, discarded. In poor countries, it happens at the production end: crops rotting in fields for lack of storage, spoiling in transit for lack of refrigeration, lost to pests for lack of infrastructure. The solutions are correspondingly different, which is a polite way of saying that a British supermarket throwing away cosmetically imperfect carrots and a Kenyan farmer watching grain rot in an unventilated shed are experiencing the same statistic from opposite ends, and neither benefits from being told that the other exists.

The species spends approximately $2.2 trillion per year on military expenditure. The FAO estimates that ending hunger entirely would cost roughly $33 billion per year in additional investment. The ratio is sixty-seven to one. We spend sixty-seven times more on the capacity to destroy things than we would need to spend to ensure that every human being on the planet has enough to eat. This is not an editorial comment. It is a ratio. It is, when stated with sufficient precision, its own commentary.

## What the Trend Line Holds

The line on the chart bends downward. Not smoothly. Not without reversals. Not without the stall at 2015 and the spike at 2020 and the stubborn regional disparities that make the global average a statistical convenience rather than a lived experience. But the line bends downward.

In 2000, one in seven people did not have enough to eat. In 2024, the figure is closer to one in eleven. Hundreds of millions of individual lives crossed a threshold -- not from hunger to abundance, but from not enough to something closer to enough. The school lunches helped. The agricultural science helped. The mobile phones that reorganised supply chains helped. The boring, incremental, unglamorous work of improving storage and roads and irrigation -- the kind of work that has never once gone viral -- helped most of all.

The question that remains is not whether progress is possible. The question is whether the species can sustain it through climate disruption, through conflict, through the particular human talent for looking away at precisely the wrong moment. We grow enough food for ten billion. We are eight billion. The gap between what we produce and what we deliver is not a mystery of science or a failure of agriculture.

It is a choice we keep making.
