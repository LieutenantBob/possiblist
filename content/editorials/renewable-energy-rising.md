The universe is roughly 13.8 billion years old. For most of that time, the dominant energy source was gravity -- pulling hydrogen together until it fused, which is an extraordinarily violent way to generate light but, in fairness, nobody had proposed an alternative. Stars ran on fusion for billions of years without complaint or regulatory review. Then, on a small rocky planet orbiting an unremarkable star in an unremarkable arm of an unremarkable galaxy, a species of recently bipedal apes discovered that if you burned ancient compressed plants, things got warm. They did this for about three hundred years, with increasing enthusiasm and decreasing air quality, until some of them noticed that the original energy source -- the one that had been running the universe since before the planet existed -- was still right there, falling on their rooftops for free.

It took them a while to look up.

When Gapminder asked people what share of the world's electricity comes from renewable sources, sixty per cent got it wrong, and the most popular wrong answer was fifteen per cent. The actual figure is roughly thirty per cent, which is double what most people believe, and which has been climbing so quickly that even the people paid to forecast it have spent the last two decades revising their projections upward with the quiet embarrassment of someone who keeps having to redo a maths problem on the whiteboard. I would have guessed low too. I suspect you would have as well. This is not because we are inattentive. It is because the thing that happened is the kind of thing that does not happen in the time frame in which it happened, except that it did.

There is a chart that the International Energy Agency publishes periodically, comparing its past projections of solar growth to what actually occurred. It is, to anyone who enjoys watching confident institutions be wrong, one of the great comic documents of our time. Every projection -- every single one, from 2002 through 2018 -- predicted a gentle rise. Every single one was overtaken by reality within two to four years. The IEA kept drawing straight lines. The sun kept drawing a curve. If solar energy were a stock and the IEA were the analyst, someone would have been quietly reassigned to cover utilities.

And here is the thing about exponential curves: they feel slow until they feel fast, and by the time they feel fast, most of the story has already happened. A third of the world's electricity is now renewable, up from a fifth in 2010. That sentence contains fewer words than most people need to process what it means.

## The Price That Fell Off a Cliff and Kept Falling

In 1977, a single watt of solar photovoltaic capacity cost seventy-six dollars. Today it costs less than thirty cents. This is a decline of more than ninety-nine per cent, which is the kind of statistic that should, by rights, be painted on the side of buildings. Instead, it has been quietly filed away in energy reports that no one reads at parties, which is a shame, because it is arguably the most encouraging price chart in human history.

The phenomenon has a name -- Swanson's Law -- after Richard Swanson, the founder of SunPower Corporation, who observed in the early 2000s that the price of solar cells drops roughly twenty per cent for every doubling of cumulative manufacturing capacity. This is not a physical law. It is not etched into the fabric of the universe. It is a learning curve, the same kind that made Model T Fords affordable and pocket calculators cheaper than the cardboard they were packaged in. The difference is speed. The Ford production curve played out over decades. The calculator curve over about fifteen years. The solar curve has been running for nearly fifty years and shows no sign of flattening, which is the sort of behaviour that makes energy economists develop a nervous tic near their left eye.

Nobody predicted this. Or rather, some people predicted it, and they were ignored with the particular confidence that institutions reserve for predictions involving exponential growth. The fossil fuel industry's own forecasts are worth examining not because they were wrong -- everyone's forecasts were wrong -- but because they were wrong in a consistent and revealing direction. Shell's 2013 "New Lens Scenarios" projected that solar might reach significant deployment by 2060. BP's 2011 Energy Outlook gave renewables a politely marginal role through 2030. ExxonMobil's 2012 projections did not include a scenario in which solar energy mattered at all, which is the energy-sector equivalent of Kodak's 1975 assessment that digital photography would probably remain a laboratory curiosity.

## The Countries That Did Not Wait for Permission

Denmark, a country with the land area of a large American county and the weather temperament of a disappointed librarian, now generates more than half its electricity from wind. It did not do this because it was sunny. It did this because it was stubborn. The Danish wind programme began in the 1970s after the oil crisis, when the country decided that being dependent on foreign fossil fuels was, on balance, undignified. Forty years later, the turbines in the North Sea are the size of skyscrapers, and on particularly blustery days, Denmark produces so much wind power that it exports the surplus to Germany, which is the kind of quiet Nordic triumph that nobody writes action films about.

Costa Rica has run on more than ninety-eight per cent renewable electricity for over a decade, primarily from hydropower, geothermal, and wind. The fact that a small Central American country managed this while much richer nations were still debating whether the transition was economically feasible is, if nothing else, a useful corrective to the assumption that clean energy is a luxury only wealthy countries can afford.

And then there is Morocco's Noor solar complex, which sits at the edge of the Sahara near Ouarzazate -- the same landscape where they filmed parts of *Gladiator* and *Game of Thrones*, both stories set in civilisations that eventually collapsed, which is either poetic or ominous depending on your disposition. Noor is one of the largest concentrated solar power plants on Earth, using parabolic mirrors to heat molten salt to over five hundred degrees Celsius, which then generates steam after sunset. Morocco imports over ninety per cent of its fossil fuel energy. Rather than accept this as a permanent condition, it built a power station that stores sunlight in liquid salt. This is not, when you describe it precisely, a sentence that should exist. But it does.

## The Duck and the Battery

The transition has a mascot, and it is a duck.

The "duck curve" is what happens to electricity demand in a grid with a lot of solar power. During the middle of the day, solar floods the system, pushing net demand from conventional power plants very low -- the belly of the duck. Then, around five or six in the evening, the sun sets, everyone turns on their cookers and televisions, and demand from conventional sources rockets upward -- the neck of the duck. The resulting shape on the demand chart looks, if you squint and have been working in grid management too long, like a duck. California's grid operator, CAISO, first published the duck curve in 2013. By 2020, the duck was so pronounced that California was routinely curtailing -- that is, deliberately wasting -- solar power in the middle of the day because there was nowhere to put it.

The solution is storage, and storage is having its own Swanson's Law moment. Lithium-ion battery costs have fallen by more than ninety per cent since 2010. Grid-scale battery installations doubled globally in 2023. California, which had essentially zero grid batteries in 2019, now has enough to power several million homes for four hours. The duck is being tamed, not by eliminating the curve but by teaching the grid to save the belly for the neck, which is the kind of sentence that only makes sense if you have spent too long thinking about waterfowl-shaped demand charts, and which is nonetheless exactly what is happening.

## The Country That Is Both the Problem and the Answer

Any honest discussion of the energy transition must eventually arrive at China, and when it does, it must hold two entirely contradictory facts in the same hand without flinching.

China is the world's largest emitter of carbon dioxide. It burns more coal than the rest of the world combined. Its emissions are still rising. In 2023, it approved the construction of new coal plants at a rate that made climate advocates physically ill.

China also installed more solar capacity in 2023 than the entire world had installed in any single year before 2022. It manufactures over eighty per cent of the world's solar panels. It dominates global battery production. It has more installed wind capacity than any other country. It is building high-voltage transmission lines across distances that would make a European grid engineer weep. In the first quarter of 2024, clean energy sources generated more electricity in China than coal for the first time in the country's history.

This is not a contradiction that resolves neatly. China is simultaneously the largest obstacle to and the largest engine of the global energy transition. It is the factory floor of the clean-energy revolution and the world's biggest customer for the product the revolution is meant to replace. If this feels confusing, that is because it is confusing. The world's most important energy story does not have a hero and a villain. It has one country playing both roles at once, in the same fiscal year, sometimes in the same policy document.

## What the Good News Does Not Tell You

A possiblist holds two things at once, and does not flinch.

The thirty per cent figure includes hydropower, which has been generating electricity since the 1880s and accounts for roughly half of the renewable total. Strip out hydro, and solar and wind specifically account for about fifteen per cent of global electricity -- still remarkable, still growing fast, but a less triumphant number than the headline suggests. The distinction matters because hydro is largely built out in the developed world; the growth story is almost entirely a solar and wind story.

Electricity, meanwhile, is only about twenty per cent of total final energy consumption. Transport runs on petroleum. Industrial heat runs on natural gas and coal. Home heating, in much of the world, runs on whatever is available and affordable. The transition in electricity generation is real and accelerating. The transition in total energy use has barely begun. The thirty per cent figure describes a genuine revolution in one-fifth of the energy system. The other four-fifths remain largely untouched, which is the sort of footnote that should appear in larger type.

Intermittency is real. The sun does not shine at night. The wind does not blow on command. Grid-scale storage is improving rapidly but is not yet deployed at the scale needed to run a modern industrial economy on variable renewables alone. The materials required for batteries and solar panels -- lithium, cobalt, rare earth elements -- come with their own extraction costs, environmental disruption, and geopolitical dependencies. Replacing one set of resource entanglements with another is progress, but it is not simplicity.

And then there is the hardest part. Roughly 675 million people on Earth still lack access to electricity altogether. For them, the question is not whether the grid runs on coal or solar. The question is whether there is a grid. Energy poverty does not care about the duck curve. The most efficient path out of extreme deprivation has, historically, involved burning fossil fuels, because fossil fuels are dense, portable, and available. Telling a country with no reliable electricity that it should leapfrog to renewables is easy to say from a country that industrialised on coal. Whether it is possible to do -- to electrify the remaining unelectrified world without a significant fossil fuel bridge -- is a genuine open question, and anyone who tells you the answer is obvious is selling something.

## The Curve and the Clock

The fossil fuel era is not over. It is not close to over by the measures that matter most -- total primary energy, cumulative emissions, atmospheric concentration of CO2. The atmospheric clock does not care about market share percentages. It cares about gigatons, and the gigatons are still accumulating, every year, in a direction that is, by any reasonable assessment, alarming.

But the shape of the curve has changed. The question is no longer whether renewables can compete with fossil fuels on cost. They can, and in most of the world, they already win. The question is no longer whether the transition will happen. It is happening, faster than anyone predicted, including the optimists, including the people who built the solar panels, including the IEA analysts who keep having to sharpen their pencils and redraw their projections a little higher, a little steeper, a little sooner.

The question now is whether it will happen fast enough. That is a different kind of question. It is not a question about technology, or economics, or even politics, though it involves all three. It is a question about time -- about whether an exponential curve that is moving in the right direction is moving in the right direction quickly enough to matter against a clock that does not negotiate and does not wait.

There is a solar panel factory in China that produces one panel every two seconds. There is a wind turbine in the North Sea that powers a town. There is a concentrated solar plant in Morocco that stores sunlight in molten salt and releases it after dark. There is a lithium-ion battery in California that absorbs the midday surplus and returns it at dinner time. None of these things existed, in any meaningful sense, thirty years ago.

The species that took three hundred years to notice the sun was still there is now, finally, in a considerable hurry.

It could still be too late. It might not be. A possiblist notices the curve.
