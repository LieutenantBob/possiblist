For most of the time that human beings have existed, which is a period considerably longer than most human beings appreciate, the overwhelming majority of them could not read. This is worth pausing on. The species that would eventually produce the Rosetta Stone, the Dead Sea Scrolls, the complete works of Shakespeare, the IKEA assembly manual, and the terms and conditions that nobody has ever read for any software product in history, spent roughly ninety-five per cent of its literate existence in a state where literacy was the possession of a vanishingly small elite. Priests could read. Scribes could read. The occasional merchant who needed to keep accounts could read. Everyone else got by on memory, word of mouth, and the general assumption that if something were truly important, someone would eventually shout it.

When Gapminder asked people across multiple countries what share of the world's adults can read and write, sixty-eight per cent got it wrong. The most common guess was sixty-five per cent, which would have been a reasonable answer in approximately 1985 and has not been accurate for forty years. I would like to report that I did better. I did not. My own estimate, offered with the quiet confidence of a person who reads for a living, was seventy-five per cent. I was wrong by the population of Europe.

The actual figure is eighty-seven per cent. Nearly seven out of every eight adults on the planet can now read and write. In 1950, the number was thirty-six per cent. In 1960, it was forty-two. The species went from a world where most adults could not read a bus timetable, a medicine label, or their own name on a legal document, to a world where the substantial majority can, in roughly the time it takes for a single human life to unfold. If you are reading this sentence, you are participating in what is, statistically, one of the most dramatic expansions of a cognitive skill in the history of any species on any planet we have yet discovered, which admittedly remains a sample size of one, but it is a striking data point nonetheless.

To feel the scale of this: in 1950, there were roughly 2.5 billion people on Earth, and about 1.6 billion of them could not read the very newspaper that might have reported this fact. Today, the global population has tripled, yet the absolute number of literate adults has increased roughly sevenfold. The world got much bigger and much more readable at the same time, which is not how most people imagine the twentieth century went.

And here is where the story becomes genuinely interesting. Because the question of how the species taught itself to read -- at scale, across every continent, in hundreds of languages, many of which had to be substantially redesigned for the purpose -- turns out to involve a man who changed an entire nation's alphabet over a single weekend, a technology that the Catholic Church initially regarded as an instrument of the devil, and the single most consistent finding in all of development economics.

## The Technology That Terrified the Authorities

Before literacy could spread, the written word had to become cheap. For most of history, it was not. A single book in medieval Europe could take a scribe months to produce. The resulting object cost roughly as much as a house. Libraries were chained to their shelves, literally -- the books, not the libraries -- because replacing a stolen volume represented a financial catastrophe roughly equivalent to losing a mid-range automobile in today's terms, except that you could not insure a book and the deductible was infinite.

Johannes Gutenberg changed this around 1440, and the speed of the change is itself instructive. Within fifty years of the printing press's invention, an estimated twenty million volumes had been produced in Europe. By 1600, the number was between one hundred and fifty and two hundred million. The price of a book fell by roughly eighty per cent in the first century of printing. This is, if you adjust for the drama of the metaphor, the fifteenth-century version of the internet: a technology that made the reproduction of information so cheap that the existing institutions built around information scarcity -- the Church, the monasteries, the scriptoria -- found themselves in the position of a guild of candlemakers watching someone wire a city for electricity.

The Catholic Church's initial response to the printing press was, in hindsight, exactly what you would predict from an institution whose authority rested partly on being the intermediary between a Latin text and a population that could not read it. Martin Luther's Ninety-Five Theses were printed and distributed across Germany in two weeks. The Church had spent fifteen hundred years as the primary bottleneck between written knowledge and the human mind. Gutenberg removed the bottleneck. The Reformation followed, which was, among other things, a literacy event disguised as a religious one.

## The Man Who Changed the Alphabet on a Tuesday

Of all the methods by which nations have pursued literacy, none is quite as breathtaking in its audacity as what Mustafa Kemal Ataturk did to Turkey in 1928.

The problem was structural. Ottoman Turkish was written in Arabic script, which is extraordinarily beautiful and, for the Turkish language, extraordinarily impractical. Turkish is an agglutinative language -- words are built by stacking suffixes -- and the Arabic script, which omits most vowels, rendered Turkish text into something that even literate Turks found genuinely difficult to read fluently. Ottoman literacy rates hovered around ten to fifteen per cent, and the script itself was part of the reason.

Ataturk's solution was not to reform the script. It was to abolish it. On 1 November 1928, the Turkish Grand National Assembly adopted a new Latin-based alphabet designed specifically for Turkish phonology. The old script was banned from official use. Ataturk personally toured the country with a blackboard, teaching the new letters in public squares. Government employees were given three months to learn the new alphabet or face dismissal. The entire written culture of a nation of fifteen million people was replaced in a matter of months.

This is the kind of policy that, described in a textbook, sounds like a historical curiosity. Described precisely, it is one of the most extraordinary acts of social engineering ever attempted by a government on its own population. Turkey's literacy rate went from roughly ten per cent to over thirty per cent within a decade, and continued climbing. Today it stands at approximately ninety-seven per cent. Whether the method was admirable or authoritarian -- and it was, rather conspicuously, both -- the results are difficult to argue with.

## One Generation: The South Korean Proof

If Turkey's transformation was dramatic, South Korea's was something closer to impossible.

In 1945, when Korea emerged from thirty-five years of Japanese colonial rule, mass illiteracy was the norm. The Japanese colonial education system had been conducted in Japanese, and Korean-language education had been suppressed. The country was devastated, partitioned, and about to be devastated again. Within one generation -- a single human lifetime -- South Korea went from mass illiteracy to a literacy rate of ninety-nine per cent. It simultaneously went from one of the poorest countries on Earth to one of the richest, which is not a coincidence, though the relationship between literacy and prosperity is more complicated than the simple version of the story suggests.

What South Korea had was Hangul, a writing system so elegant in its design that linguists regularly describe it as the most rational alphabet ever created. King Sejong the Great commissioned it in the fifteenth century specifically because the Chinese characters then in use were too difficult for common people to learn. The system is featural -- the shapes of the consonant letters literally depict the position of the tongue and lips when pronouncing them. A motivated learner can acquire the basics of Hangul in a matter of hours. This is not an exaggeration deployed for rhetorical effect. UNESCO gives an annual literacy prize named after King Sejong, which is the closest the international community has come to formally acknowledging that some writing systems are better designed than others, a truth that diplomacy normally requires everyone to pretend is not the case.

## The Finding That Changed Everything

And here is the thing that really gets you. Every development metric improves when women learn to read.

This is not a poetic generalisation. It is one of the most robustly documented findings in the history of social science. When female literacy rises, child mortality falls. When female literacy rises, birth rates decline. When female literacy rises, children stay in school longer, household nutrition improves, vaccination rates increase, and GDP per capita grows. The effect is so consistent and so large that some economists have argued that if you could only fund one intervention in a developing country -- one single programme, with all the others cancelled -- you should fund girls' education, and most of the other indicators would eventually follow.

In 1950, roughly half as many women as men could read worldwide. The female literacy rate was approximately twenty-five per cent. Today it is eighty-three per cent. The gap has not closed entirely -- it persists most stubbornly in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia -- but it has narrowed from a chasm to a crack, and the crack is still narrowing. UNESCO's global campaigns, beginning with the Experimental World Literacy Programme in the 1960s and continuing through the Education for All initiative in 1990 and the Sustainable Development Goals in 2015, have made female literacy an explicit target, not because it is fashionable, but because the data made it impossible to justify any other priority.

Consider that in 1950, the majority of human beings could not have read the very newspaper article announcing that the majority of human beings could not read. There is something almost poetically recursive about an illiteracy crisis that cannot, by definition, be communicated to the people experiencing it. The fact that this recursion has been largely resolved -- that the species can now, for the first time in its history, mostly talk to itself in writing -- is the sort of milestone that deserves more attention than it has received, which is almost none.

## What the Headline Number Does Not Tell You

A possiblist holds the complication without dropping the progress, and the progress without ignoring the complication.

The eighty-seven per cent figure measures basic literacy -- the ability to read and write a simple statement about one's own life. This is the threshold UNESCO uses, and it is a reasonable starting point, but it is also a very low bar. Functional literacy -- the ability to actually use reading and writing in daily life, to comprehend a lease agreement, to follow written medical instructions, to evaluate the credibility of a news article -- is harder to measure and almost certainly lower than the headline number suggests.

In the United States, a 2020 Department of Education survey found that roughly twenty-one per cent of American adults read at or below a "Level 1" standard, meaning they could locate a single piece of information in a short text but struggled with anything more complex. In the United Kingdom, the figure is similar. In France, roughly seven per cent of adults are classified as functionally illiterate. These are rich countries with universal compulsory education and libraries on every other corner. The gap between "can decode a sentence" and "can use written language as a tool for navigating the world" turns out to be vast, and it exists everywhere.

Then there is the digital literacy gap, which is the twenty-first century's contribution to the problem. Being able to read a printed page is necessary but no longer sufficient. The ability to navigate a screen, to distinguish a news article from an advertisement, to identify a phishing email, to understand that a search result is not the same thing as a fact -- these are literacy skills that did not exist a generation ago and that formal education has been slow to address. A person can be literate by UNESCO's definition and functionally helpless in the information environment they actually inhabit, which is, if you think about it carefully, a new kind of the old problem wearing different clothes.

Sub-Saharan Africa remains the region where the original gap is widest. Adult literacy in Chad is approximately twenty-six per cent. In Niger, it is roughly thirty-five per cent. In South Sudan, reliable figures barely exist. The gender disparity in these regions is not a crack but a canyon: in some countries, women's literacy rates are half those of men. The global average has risen like a tide, but it has not risen evenly, and the places where it has risen least are, by no coincidence, the places where poverty, conflict, and institutional fragility are most concentrated.

## The Quiet Close

The story of global literacy is not a triumph and not a tragedy. It is, like most true stories, both at once and neither exactly.

In the span of a single century, the species went from a world where most of its members could not read to a world where most of them can. This happened through printing presses and alphabet reforms and UNESCO campaigns and village schools with tin roofs and chalk dust, through millions of teachers whose names nobody recorded doing work that nobody covered, in languages that were sometimes redesigned for the purpose. It happened so gradually that no single generation noticed the milestone as it passed.

Seven hundred and seventy million adults still cannot read. That number is large. It is also the smallest it has ever been, in both absolute terms and as a share of the population. Both of these facts are true. A possiblist does not choose between them.

Most of the world can now read this sentence. That is new.
