There is a test that Hans Rosling used to administer to audiences of all kinds — business leaders, politicians, Nobel laureates, journalists. He would ask them what proportion of the world's girls of primary school age were enrolled in school. The majority guessed somewhere around fifty per cent. The correct answer, then and now, is approximately ninety per cent.

The reaction was always the same: a brief, stunned silence, followed by the uneasy sensation of having been confidently wrong about something that matters rather a lot.

In 1970, just over half of primary-age girls worldwide were in school. By 2024, the figure had risen to ninety per cent. This is, by any measure, one of the most consequential social transformations in human history. It happened in two generations. It happened across every region. It happened through a combination of policy, economics, and the gradually shifting expectations of parents who themselves had never attended school.

And yet seventy-one per cent of people believe the figure is far lower. This is not a failure of education policy — it is a failure of perception. We are living in the most educated period in human history, and most educated people do not know it.

The mechanisms behind this transformation are less dramatic than the outcome suggests.

In Bangladesh, a programme that provided a small stipend to families who sent their daughters to secondary school produced results so striking that it became a template for programmes across South and Southeast Asia. The economics were straightforward: compensate families for the lost labour of an educated daughter, and enrolment rises. The secondary effects were not: educated women had fewer children, earned more, and invested more in the education and health of the next generation.

This feedback loop — education leading to lower fertility, higher income, and better health, which in turn enables more education — is sometimes called the "girl effect," and it is one of the most powerful forces for development that has ever been documented. Economists at the Brookings Institution have estimated that each additional year of schooling for girls increases their future earnings by ten to twenty per cent.

There is also a climate connection that surprises most people. Project Drawdown, the comprehensive climate solutions analysis, ranked girls' education as the sixth most impactful solution for reducing greenhouse gas emissions — above solar farms, above electric vehicles. The mechanism is demographic: educated women have fewer children, which reduces future emissions. This is not a recommendation; it is an observation about the extraordinary interconnectedness of human systems.

The caveat is real and worth stating clearly: enrolment is not the same as learning. In many of the countries where girls' enrolment has risen most dramatically, learning outcomes remain poor. A girl who attends school but cannot read by age ten has not received an education — she has received the appearance of one. The World Bank's concept of "learning poverty" — the share of ten-year-olds who cannot read a simple passage — reveals that the gap between enrolment and actual education remains wide.
