For most of the history of civilisation, the working assumption was that approximately half the species did not require a formal education. This was not phrased quite so bluntly, of course. It was dressed up in the language of natural roles and separate spheres and the gentle suggestion that girls had more important things to do, such as fetching water, minding younger siblings, and preparing to fetch water and mind siblings for the rest of their lives. The arrangement persisted for roughly five thousand years, which is, if nothing else, a testament to the human capacity for not questioning things that are obviously worth questioning.

Then, in the space of two generations, it mostly stopped.

When Gapminder asked people across fourteen countries what share of the world's primary-age girls were enrolled in school, seventy-one per cent got it wrong. Not slightly wrong, in the way you might misremember the population of Belgium. Wrong in a way that suggests the entire twentieth century happened without anyone sending a memo. The most common answer was fifty per cent. People looked at the question, thought about it for a moment, and concluded that the world had made essentially no progress on girls' education since the era of typewriters and polio.

I would like to report that I did better. I did not. I guessed sixty per cent, which is the kind of answer that feels cautiously informed and is, in fact, the global figure from approximately 1995. I was not even wrong in an interesting direction. I was wrong in the direction of thirty years ago, which is a surprisingly common place for people's mental models of the world to be parked.

The actual figure is ninety per cent. Nine out of ten girls of primary school age, worldwide, are in school. In 1970, the number was barely half. What happened between then and now is one of the largest expansions of human potential in recorded history, and it unfolded so quietly that most of the world's most educated people believe it has not yet happened.

Here is one way to feel the scale. In 1970, there were roughly 300 million primary-age girls on Earth, and about 155 million of them were in school. Today there are roughly 350 million primary-age girls, and about 315 million of them are in school. The world added 160 million girls to classrooms in fifty-four years. That is more than the entire population of Russia, added one desk at a time, in countries where desks were frequently the thing in shortest supply.

And yet here is the part that makes a possiblist lean forward. The story behind those numbers -- who fought for them, what they cost, what happened next, and why the consequences ripple through everything from child mortality to climate change -- turns out to be considerably stranger and more interconnected than the headline suggests.

## The Doors That Were Opened, and Who Opened Them

The history of girls' education is not a history of grand proclamations. It is a history of specific people in specific places deciding, often at considerable personal risk, that the prevailing arrangement was nonsense.

In 1848, a missionary named Mary Carpenter -- not the famous one, a different Mary Carpenter, which is itself a comment on how many women did consequential things and were then filed under the same name -- began establishing ragged schools for girls in Bristol. Her argument was not sentimental. It was economic. Uneducated girls became uneducated mothers, who raised children in conditions that cost the state more in poor relief than the schools would have cost in the first place. This argument, stated with Victorian precision in pamphlets that were widely circulated and narrowly read, would take approximately 120 years to become global policy. The lag between a good idea and its implementation is one of the more reliable constants in human affairs.

In British India, Savitribai Phule and her husband Jyotirao opened the first school for girls in Pune in 1848 -- the same year, an ocean away. She was pelted with stones and dung on her walk to school each morning. She carried a spare sari. The detail is worth pausing on: she anticipated the hostility with such regularity that she packed for it. The school survived. By 1851, the Phules were running three schools. Savitribai is now on Indian postage stamps, which is the nation's way of saying "we are sorry, and also you were right, and also here is a stamp."

The modern acceleration, though, begins not with individual courage but with a spreadsheet. In 1994, the government of Bangladesh launched the Female Secondary School Assistance Programme, which did something so straightforward it is almost embarrassing to describe: it paid families a small stipend if they kept their daughters in secondary school. The payment compensated for the lost labour and the delayed marriage that an educated daughter represented. The economics were not complicated. A girl in school was a girl not earning, and for families living on the margin, that arithmetic was the entire obstacle.

The results were immediate and large. Female secondary enrolment in Bangladesh roughly doubled within a decade. The programme became a template -- first for the rest of South Asia, then for sub-Saharan Africa, then for anyone who was paying attention, which was, initially, a smaller group than you might hope. The World Bank, having spent years on considerably more elaborate interventions with considerably less impressive results, studied the Bangladesh programme with the quiet intensity of someone who suspects they have been overcomplicating things.

What made the Bangladeshi experiment genuinely interesting, however, was not the enrolment figures. It was what happened next.

## The Cascade, or Why Economists Get Excited About School Desks

There is a phenomenon in development economics that has the rare distinction of being both well-documented and insufficiently famous. It works like this: when you educate girls, a series of consequences unfold that are so consistently positive, across so many countries, over so many decades, that economists have run out of cautious ways to describe them and have resorted to calling it "the single best investment in the developing world." This is not the kind of language economists use lightly. These are people who describe the Great Depression as "a significant negative demand shock."

The cascade runs roughly as follows. An educated girl marries later -- on average, about 1.5 years later for each additional year of schooling. She has fewer children -- roughly 0.3 fewer per year of education in countries with high fertility rates. Those children she does have are significantly more likely to survive past age five, because their mother can read the instructions on oral rehydration salts, recognise the symptoms of pneumonia, and navigate a health system that was designed by people who assumed all patients could read. Each of those surviving children receives more investment -- more food, more school, more vaccination -- because there are fewer of them and their mother has more resources and more knowledge to distribute.

The Brookings Institution has estimated that each additional year of schooling for a girl increases her future earnings by ten to twenty per cent. The World Bank has found that a single additional year of primary school for girls correlates with a 5 to 15 per cent reduction in child mortality. The Inter-American Development Bank discovered that mothers' education is a stronger predictor of child survival than household income, which means that the question "did her mother go to school?" is, statistically speaking, a better question to ask than "is this family poor?" That is a finding that should rearrange a few assumptions.

And then there is the part that surprises nearly everyone. Project Drawdown -- the most comprehensive analysis of climate solutions ever compiled -- ranked girls' education as the sixth most impactful intervention for reducing greenhouse gas emissions. Above solar farms. Above electric vehicles. Above nuclear power. The mechanism is demographic: educated women have fewer children, which reduces future population, which reduces future emissions. This is not a policy recommendation. It is an observation about the extraordinary interconnectedness of human systems, and it is the kind of finding that makes you want to sit down for a moment and stare at a wall.

A girl in a classroom in Bangladesh is, through a chain of causation that passes through marriage age, family size, child survival, maternal income, and the accumulated carbon footprint of humans not yet born, a more effective climate intervention than a solar panel on a roof in Hamburg. The fact that this sentence is both true and deeply strange is, in a way, the entire point.

## Country Stories, or What Progress Looks Like When You Get Close

In 1970, a girl born in South Asia had roughly a coin-flip chance of ever setting foot in a classroom. Her granddaughter today has a nine-in-ten chance. The grandmother did not make the news. Neither did the granddaughter.

The state of Kerala, in southern India, provides perhaps the most instructive example of what happens when a society commits to girls' education with genuine seriousness. In the 1950s, Kerala's communist government -- which is the kind of sentence that causes a certain type of Western commentator to short-circuit -- invested massively in public education, particularly for women and lower castes. By the 1990s, Kerala had a female literacy rate above ninety per cent, the lowest infant mortality in India, the lowest birth rate, and the highest life expectancy. It accomplished this with a per-capita income lower than most other Indian states. Kerala is not rich. It is educated, which turns out, in the long run, to be more useful.

The comparison within India is stark enough to constitute its own argument. In Bihar, where female literacy hovered around thirty per cent well into the 2000s, infant mortality was roughly four times higher than in Kerala. Same country. Same constitution. Same monsoons. Different investment in whether girls could read. The data does not require editorial comment. It requires attention.

Then there is Afghanistan, which is what happens when the cascade runs in reverse. Before the Taliban's first period of rule in the 1990s, girls in Kabul attended school, attended university, became doctors and engineers. Under Taliban rule, girls' education was banned. After the US-led intervention in 2001, girls returned to school in vast numbers -- by 2018, 3.5 million Afghan girls were enrolled. When the Taliban retook control in 2021, they banned girls' secondary education again. The results are predictable because they are the same results that occur every time the experiment is run: within two years, child marriage rates rose, maternal mortality projections worsened, and the generation of women who had trained as teachers, nurses, and civil servants found themselves forbidden to work. Afghanistan is running the controlled experiment that no ethics board would ever approve, and the results confirm exactly what the data from everywhere else already showed.

Malala Yousafzai's story is famous -- shot by the Taliban at fifteen for advocating girls' education, survived, became the youngest Nobel laureate. What is less famous, because it is less dramatic and therefore less suited to the architecture of attention, is the story of the millions of girls whose education happened without a bullet. The girl in rural Ethiopia who walked four kilometres each way. The girl in Bangladesh whose family turned down a marriage offer because the stipend made school affordable. The girl in Bihar whose mother, herself illiterate, decided that this particular pattern would not repeat. These girls did not win Nobel Prizes. They got jobs. They had fewer children. Their children survived at higher rates. They are the cascade, operating at the scale of individual decisions made in places where the news does not go.

## The Absurd Economics

Here is a number that should be printed on the wall of every finance ministry on Earth: the World Bank estimates that limited educational opportunities for girls cost countries between $15 trillion and $30 trillion in lost lifetime productivity and earnings. That is not a typo, and it is not an abstract projection. It is the measured gap between what women earn with education and what they earn without it, aggregated globally.

For comparison, the entire annual GDP of the United States is approximately $28 trillion. The world is leaving on the table, every generation, roughly the equivalent of the American economy -- because a portion of its girls still do not complete secondary school. If a corporation were leaving this much value unrealised, the board would be replaced. When a species does it, it is called tradition.

The cost of closing the gap is, by contrast, almost comically small. The Global Partnership for Education estimates that providing quality education for every girl in the developing world would cost an additional $26 billion per year. Global military spending exceeds $2.4 trillion. The ratio is instructive: the world currently spends roughly ninety-two times more on the capacity to destroy things than it would need to spend to educate every girl on the planet. This is not an editorial comment. It is a ratio. But it is a ratio that rewards a moment of quiet reflection.

## What the Good News Does Not Tell You

A possiblist holds two things at once, and does not flinch.

Enrolment is not attendance, and attendance is not learning. This is not a footnote. It is the central challenge of the next generation of education policy. The World Bank's measure of "learning poverty" -- the share of ten-year-olds who cannot read a simple passage -- reveals a gap that the enrolment figures conceal. In sub-Saharan Africa, where girls' primary enrolment has risen dramatically, learning poverty exceeds sixty per cent. A girl who sits in a classroom for six years and emerges unable to read a sentence has not received an education. She has received the appearance of one, which is an altogether different thing and not a minor distinction.

The secondary school gap is where the story gets complicated. While primary enrolment has reached ninety per cent, girls' secondary completion in the poorest countries remains below forty per cent. The reasons are specific and reinforcing: early marriage, menstruation without sanitation facilities, the sheer distance to the nearest secondary school in rural areas, and the economic calculation that a fourteen-year-old girl's labour or marriage price is worth more to the family than whatever abstract benefit further schooling might provide. The calculation is rational within its constraints, which is precisely what makes it so difficult to overcome from outside those constraints.

Child marriage remains the single largest structural barrier to girls' secondary education. Twelve million girls per year are married before the age of eighteen. That is roughly thirty-three thousand per day, a number that is both statistically significant and, when you think about what it means for each of those thirty-three thousand individuals, something else entirely. In Niger, where the child marriage rate exceeds seventy-five per cent, girls' secondary completion is below ten per cent. The correlation is not subtle.

And then there is quality. A school with no textbooks, no trained teachers, and no electricity is a school in name. The UNESCO Institute for Statistics estimates that six out of ten children and adolescents worldwide -- roughly 617 million -- are not achieving minimum proficiency in reading and mathematics, even though many of them are enrolled and attending. The building exists. The learning does not always follow.

## The View from Here

In 1970, the human species had decided, through a combination of custom, economics, and the comfortable inertia of five thousand years of precedent, that roughly half its children did not need to learn to read. By 2024, that figure had fallen to ten per cent. The remaining ten per cent are concentrated in the poorest, most fragile, most conflict-affected places on Earth, which means that the last mile of progress will be harder than everything that came before it, because it always is.

The question that the data leaves open is not whether educating girls is worthwhile. The evidence on that point is so overwhelming that continuing to debate it is itself a misallocation of resources. The question is why, given that the returns are so large and the costs so small and the evidence so unambiguous, it took until the late twentieth century for the world to try it in earnest. What other interventions are sitting in plain sight, supported by evidence, affordable at scale, and ignored because they do not fit the way we have always done things?

A possiblist asks that question. Not with despair, and not with false cheer. With the particular kind of curiosity that comes from discovering that the answer was available all along.

In 1970, it was a coin flip. Now it is nine in ten.
