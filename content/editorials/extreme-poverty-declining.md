There is a particular kind of wrongness that deserves its own category. It is not the wrongness of guessing badly, or of never having considered the question, or of being approximately right but off by a reasonable margin. It is the wrongness of believing, with quiet confidence, the precise opposite of what is true.

When Gapminder asked twelve thousand people across fourteen countries what had happened to global extreme poverty, seventy-four per cent got it wrong. That is worse than random chance. A chimpanzee throwing darts at a board would, statistically, outperform the human race on this question. This is not an insult to chimpanzees. It is an observation about the peculiar commitment humans have to believing that things are getting worse.

The truth is that the share of humanity living in extreme poverty has fallen from thirty-six per cent in 1990 to approximately ten per cent today. This is the largest reduction in human deprivation in recorded history. Roughly a hundred and fifteen thousand people left extreme poverty behind on any given day, every day, for thirty-five years. It happened in every region of the world. It happened through a combination of economic growth, targeted intervention, and the quiet accumulation of small improvements in agriculture, health, and infrastructure that no individual newspaper article could quite capture.

And yet we missed it. Not through any fault of our own, exactly, but through the very reasonable tendency to notice catastrophe and overlook gradual improvement. Good news, it turns out, is structurally invisible.

The question of why we missed it is, in some ways, more interesting than the fact itself.

Hans Rosling, the Swedish physician and statistician who spent the last decades of his life trying to correct this exact misperception, had a theory. He called it the "negativity instinct" — the human tendency to notice the bad more than the good, to remember the crisis and forget the slow, undramatic improvement. He was gentle about it. He pointed out that this is not stupidity; it is a feature of how human attention works. We evolved to notice threats, not trends.

But there is another layer to the story. The poverty line itself — the threshold below which a person is counted as "extremely poor" — is a constructed thing. It was set at $1.90 per day in 2015 (since revised upward to $2.15 in 2022 and again in 2025), and it represents a genuine attempt to measure something real: the level of income below which a person cannot reliably meet basic needs. But the line is also, inevitably, an oversimplification. Someone earning $2.20 a day has crossed the line but has not entered anything most people would recognise as prosperity.

This matters because it changes the texture of the good news. The headline figure — poverty has nearly halved — is true and important. But the lived experience of the people behind that figure is more complicated than the statistic suggests. Many of those who left extreme poverty did not leap into the middle class; they moved from desperate poverty to slightly less desperate poverty. This is progress. It is also not yet enough.

Sub-Saharan Africa tells a different version of the story entirely. While the global rate fell, the absolute number of people in extreme poverty in Africa rose — from roughly 278 million in 1990 to over 400 million by 2019. The rate fell, but the population grew faster. This is the kind of nuance that makes the good news more honest, and also more urgent.

What Rosling understood, and what the data confirms, is that both things are true at once. The world has made extraordinary progress. The world still has an extraordinary amount of work to do. A possiblist holds both facts simultaneously and proceeds accordingly.

But here is the thing that nags: if a hundred and fifteen thousand people can leave extreme poverty every day for thirty-five years, and the vast majority of educated, news-reading, well-intentioned people have absolutely no idea it happened — what else might be changing, right now, that we cannot see?
