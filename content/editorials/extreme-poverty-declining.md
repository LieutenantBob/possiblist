There is a particular kind of wrongness that deserves its own category. It is not the wrongness of guessing badly, or of never having considered the question, or of being approximately right but off by a reasonable margin. It is the wrongness of believing, with quiet confidence, the precise opposite of what is true.

When Gapminder asked twelve thousand people across fourteen countries what had happened to global extreme poverty, seventy-four per cent got it wrong. Not approximately wrong. Precisely, confidently, in-the-direction-of-the-exact-opposite wrong. A chimpanzee throwing darts at a board would, statistically, outperform the human race on this question. This is not an insult to chimpanzees. It is an observation about the peculiar commitment humans have to believing that things are getting worse.

Hans Rosling liked to test this on the most important people he could find. He gave the quiz to heads of state at Davos. They scored worse than the chimps. He gave it to Nobel laureates at a dinner in Sweden. They averaged two correct out of twelve. Random chance would have given them four. He tested the staff of the United Nations -- the very people whose job it was to know these numbers -- and found they performed only marginally better than the general public.

The truth is that the share of humanity living in extreme poverty has fallen from thirty-six per cent in 1990 to approximately ten per cent today. This is the largest reduction in human deprivation in recorded history. On any average day over the past thirty-five years, roughly a hundred and thirty thousand people left extreme poverty behind. Every day. Including Tuesdays. Especially the ones where the news was particularly grim.

It happened in every region of the world, through a combination of economic growth, targeted intervention, and the quiet accumulation of small improvements in agriculture, health, and infrastructure that no individual newspaper article could quite capture. A newspaper could have run the headline "130,000 PEOPLE ESCAPED EXTREME POVERTY TODAY" every single day for twenty-five years and it would have been true every single day. It was never a headline.

And that, perhaps, is the most interesting part of the story. Not the progress itself -- although the progress is extraordinary -- but the fact that we missed it entirely. How did the greatest improvement in human material conditions in the history of the species occur during our lifetimes, and nobody noticed?

The answer, it turns out, involves a Swedish doctor with a box of props, a peculiar feature of how human attention works, and an Australian economist who had to decide how much a dollar is worth in a country where dollars are not used.

## How to Measure Something That Looks Different Everywhere

Before you can say poverty has declined, you have to agree on what poverty is. This turns out to be the kind of problem that has kept economists employed for decades, which is, if you think about it, a mildly ironic use of resources.

The man who drew the line is Martin Ravallion, an Australian economist who arrived at the World Bank in 1988 with a question that sounds simple and is not: how do you compare poverty in Bangladesh and Bolivia when a dollar buys a meal in one and a napkin in the other? You cannot simply convert currencies at the exchange rate. A person earning the Bolivian equivalent of two dollars a day is having a meaningfully different experience from a person earning two actual dollars in Boston, where two dollars will get you approximately halfway to a small coffee.

Ravallion's solution had the elegant desperation of a man who needed an answer by Thursday. He collected national poverty lines from the world's poorest countries and noticed they clustered around one dollar per day in purchasing-power-parity terms. His logic: if the governments of the world's poorest nations themselves define poverty at roughly this level, it is a reasonable floor. It is also, he would be the first to admit, a somewhat arbitrary number arrived at by averaging the educated guesses of statistical offices that were themselves operating on shoestring budgets. The irony of measuring poverty using institutions that are themselves underfunded appears to have occurred to everyone involved.

The number has been revised several times since -- from $1 to $1.25 to $1.90 to $2.15 -- each time not because of inflation in any ordinary sense, but because the entire global price survey was redone from scratch. The International Comparison Program periodically sends researchers to compare the prices of hundreds of goods across every country on Earth, including items that exist in one place but not another. How do you price a camel ride in Norway? You impute it, which is the economist's way of saying you make something up, but with a formula. The entire system of global poverty measurement is, when you look closely, a magnificent feat of statistical improvisation held together by peer review and the shared understanding that the alternative -- not measuring it at all -- would be worse.

Angus Deaton, the Nobel laureate from Princeton, has described the resulting numbers as "not wrong, but not right either," which is the most Princeton sentence ever uttered about anything.

## The Countries That Changed Everything

The single biggest poverty reduction in history happened in China, and the mechanism was so simple it is almost embarrassing.

Between 1981 and 2015, roughly eight hundred million Chinese people crossed above the extreme poverty line. The rate fell from eighty-eight per cent to less than one per cent. To put this in perspective: China lifted more people out of poverty in thirty-five years than the entire population of Europe. If poverty reduction were an Olympic sport, everyone else would be competing for silver.

What actually happened was this. In 1978, Deng Xiaoping introduced the Household Responsibility System, which allowed farmers to sell surplus crops for profit. That is the whole thing. Let farmers keep what they grew beyond the state quota. Agricultural output jumped by roughly twenty-five per cent in a few years. Not because of new seeds. Not because of foreign aid. Not because of a grand ideological revelation. Because people were allowed to sell their own rice. It is the kind of policy that, stated plainly, makes you wonder what anyone thought the alternative was achieving.

The special economic zones that followed turned a fishing village called Shenzhen into a megacity of twelve million. This is not a metaphor or a rhetorical flourish. A person born in that fishing village in 1975 could, by their forties, be living in a city with a GDP larger than Sweden's. Sweden, for its part, remained roughly the same size throughout, which is exactly the kind of steady, undramatic performance that gets you featured on nobody's list of miracles.

Vietnam tells an almost suspiciously similar story. After the Doi Moi reforms of 1986, the country went from importing rice to being the world's second-largest rice exporter within fifteen years. Same land. Same people. Same monsoons. Just a different set of rules about who gets to sell what. Vietnam also became the world's second-largest coffee producer in the same period, going from essentially zero coffee exports to a global powerhouse. The beans, one feels, had been waiting.

India's transformation was slower, louder, and considerably more chaotic, which is a reasonable summary of most things that happen in India. The 1991 liberalisation under Narasimha Rao and a young finance minister named Manmohan Singh dismantled the "License Raj" -- a system under which starting a business required navigating a tower of bureaucratic permits so magnificently labyrinthine that obtaining the permits had itself become a thriving sector of the economy. Roughly two hundred and seventy million Indians left extreme poverty behind between 1990 and 2015. The unevenness is stark: Kerala now looks like a middle-income country with excellent literacy rates and a communist government that somehow coexists with a booming tech sector, while parts of Bihar look like Kerala would if you removed the literacy, the tech sector, and most of the roads. But the direction, even in Bihar, is unmistakable.

Bangladesh presents a paradox that anyone fond of simple moral stories will find inconvenient. The garment industry -- four million workers, eighty per cent women, at wages that no Western consumer would accept and in conditions that have, infamously, killed people -- has been one of the most powerful engines of poverty reduction and female economic empowerment in the developing world. Before the factories, most of these women had no independent income at all. The shift from zero to low transformed everything: marriage dynamics, family planning, girls' school enrolment. Studies show that areas near garment factories saw significant increases in girls staying in school. This is not comfortable. It is also not simple. Reality, as a general policy, refuses to be.

## The Man Who Saved a Billion Lives and Absolutely Nobody Knows His Name

There is a game you can play at dinner parties, if you attend the sort of dinner parties where this would be considered entertainment. Ask: who is the single person who has saved the most lives in human history? People will guess Jonas Salk, or Alexander Fleming, or -- if they are trying to be clever -- the person who invented soap.

The answer is Norman Borlaug. He won the Nobel Peace Prize. He won the Congressional Gold Medal. He won the Presidential Medal of Freedom. He is credited with saving approximately one billion lives. He has roughly the name recognition of a regional insurance adjustor.

Borlaug was an American agronomist who spent the 1950s and 1960s in Mexico, developing high-yield, disease-resistant wheat varieties. This is not, it must be said, the kind of sentence that quickens the pulse. But his dwarf wheat was introduced to India and Pakistan in the mid-1960s, at a moment when both countries were genuinely staring down famine -- the kind of famine that kills millions, not the kind that makes the news for a week. Pakistan's wheat production roughly doubled between 1965 and 1970. India went from famine to self-sufficiency within a decade. The Green Revolution that Borlaug set in motion doubled global cereal production between 1960 and 2000, while the amount of land under cultivation increased by only fifteen per cent.

His own summary of his life's work: "You can't build a peaceful world on empty stomachs and human misery."

That a man who saved a billion lives is essentially unknown, while people who have saved considerably fewer are household names, is itself a tidy demonstration of everything this article is about. The quiet, incremental, unglamorous work that actually moves the world does not produce headlines. Headlines require drama. Borlaug produced wheat. Wheat, it turns out, is not dramatic. It is merely essential.

## The Phone, the Fish, and the Cow

Not all progress came from grand reforms or agricultural science. Some of it came from things that fit in your pocket.

In 2007, Robert Jensen published a study about fishermen in Kerala, India. Before mobile phones, a fisherman would sail out, catch fish, and bring them to whichever market was nearest. Sometimes the market already had plenty of fish, and the catch would rot unsold on the beach -- which is, if nothing else, an extremely inefficient way to run a fishing industry -- while buyers at the next market over had none and were eating rice again. When mobile phones arrived, the fishermen called ahead. They checked prices. They sailed to wherever the fish were needed. Waste fell. Profits rose by eight per cent. Consumer prices dropped by four per cent. The entire fishing economy of coastal Kerala was reorganised by a device that also plays Snake.

In Kenya, M-Pesa -- a mobile money system launched the same year -- allowed people without bank accounts to send and receive money by phone. A study published in *Science* estimated that M-Pesa alone lifted 194,000 Kenyan households out of poverty, primarily through increased savings and occupational choice among women. The banking system of an entire country was leapfrogged by a text message.

And then there is the cow. BRAC, the Bangladeshi organisation founded by Fazle Hasan Abed after the Liberation War, runs a programme called "Targeting the Ultra Poor." The idea is so simple it sounds like something a child would suggest: give the very poorest families a cow. Or a few goats. Along with some training and a small amount of support. That is the programme. The families do not sell the animal. They breed it. They sell the milk. They buy a second animal. Studies across multiple countries show the effects lasting years. Abed's philosophy -- "Small is beautiful, but big is necessary" -- is the kind of sentence that should probably be on more walls and fewer motivational posters.

## What the Good News Does Not Tell You

A possiblist holds two things at once, and does not flinch.

Sub-Saharan Africa complicates every celebration. While the poverty *rate* fell from fifty-seven per cent to thirty-five per cent, the *number* of people in extreme poverty rose -- from 278 million to over 430 million -- because the population grew faster than poverty declined. Africa now accounts for roughly sixty per cent of the world's extreme poor, up from fifteen per cent in 1990. The geography of poverty has shifted, dramatically and quietly, southward, while most of the world was watching China.

The poverty line itself has critics, and they are not wrong to criticise. Jason Hickel, an anthropologist at the University of Barcelona, points out that $2.15 per day is far too low to represent anything a human being would recognise as a dignified life. At his suggested ethical poverty line of $7.40 per day -- the level associated with adequate nutrition and normal life expectancy -- roughly four billion people are in poverty, and that number has barely budged. He has a point. But the trend at $2.15 is also real: fewer people are below it than were before, and that fact matters enormously to the people who crossed it, even if it does not satisfy an anthropologist in Barcelona.

COVID reversed roughly three to four years of progress, shoving an estimated seventy to a hundred million people back below the line, concentrated in the places that could least afford it. Climate change threatens more: the World Bank estimates that 132 million additional people could be pushed into extreme poverty by 2030, primarily smallholder farmers in tropical regions. The cruel arithmetic, once again: the people least responsible for the problem are the most exposed to its consequences.

## The Architecture of Not Noticing

If none of this feels familiar, do not blame yourself. Blame the architecture.

Johan Galtung and Mari Holmboe Ruge worked out the problem in 1965: events are more newsworthy if they are negative, unexpected, and involve important countries. Slow, positive, structural change in poor countries scores zero on all three criteria. It is, by the formal definition of news values, the least newsworthy thing that can happen. The most important development in the history of human welfare is, structurally, not news.

Max Roser of Our World in Data has noted that a newspaper could have run the headline "A COUNTRY THAT MIGHT HAVE HAD A FAMINE DID NOT HAVE ONE TODAY" every single day for decades. It would have been accurate. It would also have been terrible journalism. The absence of catastrophe is, by definition, invisible. This is a feature of how information works, not a bug. It is just a feature that happens to leave several billion people with a wildly inaccurate picture of the planet they live on.

Steven Pinker has observed that "things are complicated and getting slowly better in most ways while getting worse in some ways" is a sentence that has never gone viral. There is a reason. Our brains evolved to notice threats, not trends. The rustle in the bushes might be a predator. The gradual disappearance of childhood mortality, however welcome, has never eaten anyone. We are, as a species, magnificently well-equipped to detect danger and spectacularly ill-equipped to notice improvement, which is an unfortunate combination of traits for an animal that has recently gotten quite good at improving things.

## What It Would Take

Jeffrey Sachs once estimated that ending extreme poverty entirely would cost roughly $175 billion per year -- about 0.7 per cent of rich countries' GNP. The world has never come close. Global military spending, for reference, exceeds two trillion dollars annually, which means the species currently spends roughly twelve times more on the ability to destroy things than it would need to spend to ensure that nobody on the planet goes to bed hungry. This is not an editorial comment. It is a ratio.

But the interventions work, and some of them are so cheap they border on the absurd. Cash transfers -- giving poor people money, no strings, no conditions, no patronising questionnaire about how they intend to spend it -- produce lasting improvements in income and wellbeing. The recipients do not, despite the fears of every hand-wringing editorial board, drink it away. They invest it. They buy tin roofs. They start businesses. They send their children to school.

Treating children for intestinal parasites costs fifty cents per child per year and increases both school attendance and adult earnings. Fifty cents. The coin you would not bother to pick up off the pavement is, in the right place, a meaningful intervention in a child's future.

Oral rehydration therapy -- a solution of salt, sugar, and water that costs essentially nothing -- was called by The Lancet "potentially the most important medical advance of the twentieth century." It prevents death from diarrhoea, which was, until recently, one of the biggest killers of children on Earth. The cure for one of humanity's great scourges turned out to be slightly salty sugar water. If a pharmaceutical company had invented it, it would have cost four hundred dollars a dose and had a three-syllable brand name. Instead it costs pennies and saves millions of lives per year, which is precisely the kind of thing that does not get a Super Bowl advert.

The most remarkable thing about the decline of extreme poverty is not that it happened. It is that it happened and almost nobody noticed. The greatest improvement in human material conditions in the history of the species occurred during the lifetimes of people reading this sentence, and the dominant narrative remains that things are getting worse.

This is not because people are foolish. It is because the information environment was designed to sell attention, not to inform, and attention is most efficiently sold through the sensation that everything is falling apart.

A possiblist notices. That is where it starts.
