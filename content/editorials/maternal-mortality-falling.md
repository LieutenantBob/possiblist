For roughly three hundred thousand years, give or take a geological margin of error, the human species has been reproducing itself by a method that, when described with clinical precision, involves routing a new human being through a passage not obviously designed for the purpose. Evolution, which is brilliant at designing eyes and lungs and the opposable thumb, appears to have treated the birth canal as something of an afterthought — a last-minute compromise between bipedal walking and skull size that has, across the millennia, killed more women than war, famine, and most infectious diseases combined.

This is not hyperbole. For most of recorded history, childbirth was the leading cause of death for women of reproductive age. Stated plainly, this is staggering: the single act most necessary for the continuation of the species was also the one most likely to kill half its members. If a product designer had submitted this arrangement, they would have been fired.

When Gapminder asked people what had happened to the rate of women dying in childbirth since 2000, sixty-three per cent got it wrong. The most common answer was a resigned shrug — the assumption that a problem this ancient and this deeply embedded in biology had probably stayed about the same. I would have guessed the same thing. The problem feels permanent, the kind of suffering so old it has become part of the wallpaper of being human. You do not expect wallpaper to change.

It changed.

The global maternal mortality ratio has fallen from approximately 342 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2000 to roughly 223 in 2024. That is a decline of more than a third. In absolute terms, it means that every year, roughly 120,000 women survive a day that, a generation earlier, might have killed them. To put that in a scale that is difficult to ignore: it is as if the entire population of a mid-sized city — Cambridge, or Savannah, or Uppsala — is quietly not dying, every twelve months, and the rest of the world has mostly failed to notice.

The interventions behind this number are, when you learn what they are, so profoundly unglamorous that they border on the comic. And the story of how they came to be deployed — across jungles and mountains and places with no roads and no electricity — turns out to be one of the more remarkable logistical achievements of the twenty-first century, driven largely by people whose names you will never know.

## The Doctor Who Washed His Hands

Before we arrive at the present, it is worth pausing at a maternity ward in Vienna in 1847, because the story of maternal mortality cannot be told honestly without Ignaz Semmelweis, and because his story remains, after nearly two centuries, one of the most instructive parables about what happens when a correct idea meets an entrenched profession.

Semmelweis was a Hungarian obstetrician working at the Vienna General Hospital, which operated two maternity clinics. The First Clinic, staffed by doctors and medical students, had a maternal death rate of roughly ten per cent. The Second Clinic, staffed by midwives, had a rate of about four per cent. Women knew this. They begged to be admitted to the Second Clinic. Some gave birth in the street rather than enter the First.

Semmelweis noticed that the doctors were going directly from the autopsy room to the delivery room without washing their hands. The midwives did not perform autopsies. He introduced a policy of handwashing with chlorinated lime solution. The death rate in the First Clinic dropped to roughly one per cent — below even the midwives' ward.

His reward for this discovery was professional humiliation, dismissal from his post, and eventual commitment to a mental asylum, where he died at forty-seven, probably beaten by guards. The medical establishment rejected his findings for two decades. Doctors were gentlemen, and gentlemen's hands were clean by definition. The idea that they might be killing their patients was not merely incorrect; it was offensive. The offense mattered more than the deaths.

It took Pasteur and Lister and the germ theory of disease to vindicate Semmelweis, by which point thousands of additional women had died of a problem whose solution was a basin of soapy water. This is worth remembering the next time someone suggests that evidence, clearly presented, naturally prevails. It does not. It prevails eventually. The gap between "eventually" and "immediately" is measured in graves.

## The Midwife Effect

If you were to ask a global-health economist to name the single most effective intervention for reducing maternal mortality, the answer would not be a drug, a device, or a surgical technique. It would be a person: a skilled birth attendant. The phrase is clinical and uninspiring, which is fitting, because the intervention itself is clinical and uninspiring. A trained midwife, present at the birth, who can recognise the warning signs of haemorrhage, eclampsia, obstructed labour, and sepsis, and who knows when to refer and where.

This is not a breakthrough. It is a baseline. And the fact that it was unavailable to most of humanity until quite recently tells you something uncomfortable about what the species chose to fund first.

The most persuasive evidence for what a midwife network can accomplish comes from Sri Lanka, a country that, in the 1930s, had a maternal mortality ratio of roughly 1,500 per 100,000 live births — comparable to the worst-performing countries today. By the early 2000s, it had reduced that figure to approximately 30. A decline of ninety-five per cent, achieved not through wealth (Sri Lanka's GDP per capita remained modest throughout), not through expensive technology, and not through some revolutionary medical discovery, but through the systematic deployment of trained midwives to every corner of the island.

Sri Lanka built a network of rural health units, each staffed by a public health midwife responsible for a defined catchment area. The midwife knew every pregnant woman in her area. She visited them at home. She referred complications early. The system was unglamorous, methodical, and devastatingly effective. It is the kind of solution that wins no prizes because it involves no genius — just persistent, competent organisation sustained over decades. Which is, when you think about it, the hardest kind of genius there is.

## Pennies, Thousands

The specific interventions that drive down maternal mortality have, as a group, a quality that would embarrass a pharmaceutical marketing department: they are cheap.

Postpartum haemorrhage — severe bleeding after delivery — is the leading cause of maternal death worldwide, responsible for roughly a quarter of all maternal deaths. The drug that prevents it, oxytocin, costs less than a dollar per dose. The drug that treats a related complication, misoprostol, is a tablet that can be administered by a community health worker with minimal training, requires no refrigeration, and costs pennies.

Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia — dangerously high blood pressure during pregnancy, progressing to seizures — account for another twelve per cent of maternal deaths. The treatment is magnesium sulfate, a compound so cheap and so widely available that it is also used as bath salts and fertiliser. Administered intravenously during labour, it prevents eclamptic seizures with remarkable efficacy. The cost per treatment is roughly fifty cents. This is the kind of fact that, when you encounter it for the first time, produces a particular sort of silence — the silence of realising that the barrier between a woman living and dying was, in many cases, not knowledge or technology or even money, but the simple logistics of getting fifty cents' worth of a common mineral to the right place at the right time.

And here is the thing that really gets you: the interventions that saved most of these women's lives are almost comically unglamorous — a trained midwife, a clean room, a bag of the right blood type, and a surgeon who can be reached in time. These are not breakthroughs. They are basics. The twentieth century produced magnetic resonance imaging, gene therapy, robotic surgery, and artificial hearts. The twenty-first century's most effective contribution to keeping women alive during childbirth has been making sure someone competent is in the room. The gap between what medicine can do and what most women actually receive is not a gap of innovation. It is a gap of delivery.

## The Geography of Dying

The global figure — 223 per 100,000 — is an average, and averages, as any statistician will tell you with a weariness that suggests they have said it before, can obscure more than they reveal.

Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia together account for approximately eighty-six per cent of all maternal deaths worldwide. Sub-Saharan Africa alone accounts for roughly two-thirds. In South Sudan, the maternal mortality ratio exceeds 1,200 per 100,000 live births — meaning that roughly one in every eighty pregnancies ends in the mother's death. In Chad, it is similar. In Norway, it is two.

The distance between two and twelve hundred is not a medical mystery. It is not a puzzle awaiting a clever solution. The interventions exist. The knowledge exists. What does not exist, in sufficient quantity, in the places where women are dying, is the infrastructure to connect the two: roads to hospitals, hospitals with operating theatres, operating theatres with surgeons, surgeons with reliable supplies of blood and anaesthesia, and all of this available at two in the morning in the rainy season. The problem is not what to do. The problem is doing it at scale, in difficult terrain, with limited budgets, amid competing crises.

Countries affected by conflict and fragile states account for a disproportionate share of maternal deaths, which is unsurprising in the way that many unsurprising things are nevertheless worth stating plainly: it is very difficult to run a functioning maternity ward during a civil war. The places where maternal mortality has stagnated or risen are, almost without exception, the places where everything else has also fallen apart. Maternal mortality is, in this sense, a barometer of the basic functioning of a society. When the number rises, something fundamental has broken.

## The Exception That Proves Nothing Comfortable

There is one country that complicates every tidy narrative about progress, and it is not the one you would expect.

The United States is the only high-income country in the world where maternal mortality has been rising. In 2000, the US maternal mortality ratio was roughly 12 per 100,000. By 2021, it had climbed to approximately 33 — nearly tripling in two decades. During the same period, every comparable rich nation saw its rate fall or hold steady. The United States went backwards.

The numbers are worse when disaggregated by race. Black women in the United States die in childbirth at roughly three times the rate of white women. This disparity persists after controlling for income, education, and access to insurance. A Black woman with a college degree and private health insurance faces a higher risk of maternal death than a white woman without either. The data does not support the explanation that this is simply a poverty problem. It is something else, something threaded through the structures of how care is delivered and to whom, and the United States has not yet fully reckoned with what that something is.

The reasons for the overall rise are debated and likely multiple: rising rates of caesarean sections, increasing maternal age, higher prevalence of chronic conditions like obesity and hypertension, and a maternal healthcare system that, in parts of the country, has been quietly hollowed out — rural hospitals closing their obstetric units, maternity care deserts expanding across states where the nearest delivery ward is an hour's drive away. The richest country in the history of the world has managed to make childbirth more dangerous for its citizens while spending more per capita on healthcare than any other nation on Earth. This is not an editorial comment. It is a ratio, and it is not a flattering one.

## The Quiet Arithmetic

The SDG target — fewer than 70 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030 — is almost certainly out of reach at the current rate of decline. Progress is real, but it is not fast enough. The women who will die between now and the achievement of that target are not a statistical abstraction. They are individual women, in specific places, on particular days, who will bleed or seize or develop infections that a trained attendant with basic supplies could have managed. The distance between 223 and 70 is not measured in years of research or billions of dollars of pharmaceutical development. It is measured in midwives, in roads, in blood banks, in the unglamorous machinery of a health system that works.

A species that can sequence its own genome and land a robot on Mars has not yet managed to ensure that the act of bringing a new member of that species into the world does not kill the person doing it. We are closer than we have ever been. We are not close enough.

The number is 223, and it is falling.
