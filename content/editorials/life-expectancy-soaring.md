For most of human history — and by "most" we mean roughly the first hundred thousand years — the average person could expect to live to about thirty-two. This was not because nobody ever saw forty. It was because so many people died in the first few years that the average was dragged downward by a truly appalling rate of infant mortality.

By 2024, global average life expectancy stood at approximately seventy-three years. Humanity, in the space of roughly four generations, had added more years to the average lifespan than in all of previous recorded history combined. If this were a company's growth chart, investors would be suspicious.

Two-thirds of people, when asked, guess the figure is at least a decade lower than it actually is. They tend to land somewhere around sixty, which is where the world was in 1980. This is not wildly unreasonable — it merely means that most people's mental model of the world is approximately forty-five years out of date, which is, in fairness, the sort of thing that happens when you are busy living in it.

The causes of the improvement are unglamorous. Sanitation. Vaccination. Antibiotics. Clean water. Midwifery. Better nutrition. None of these sounds like a revolution. All of them, together, were the revolution.

The history of how this happened contains some genuinely remarkable characters.

Ignaz Semmelweis, a Hungarian physician working in Vienna in the 1840s, noticed that women in the maternity ward staffed by doctors were dying at five times the rate of those attended by midwives. He hypothesised that the doctors, who came directly from performing autopsies, were carrying something on their hands. He introduced hand-washing with chlorinated lime. The death rate plummeted.

His colleagues responded by destroying his career. He was dismissed from his position, mocked in medical journals, and eventually committed to an asylum, where he died at forty-seven — possibly beaten by guards. His vindication came only after his death, when Louis Pasteur and Joseph Lister established germ theory. It is one of the more depressing case studies in how humanity treats people who are right too early.

Meanwhile, in London, a retired military engineer named Joseph Bazalgette was designing the sewer system that would end cholera in the city. He was not a doctor. He was not a scientist. He was an engineer who understood that if sewage entered the drinking water supply, people died, and that the solution was not medical but infrastructural. His sewers still operate today, which is more than can be said for most Victorian innovations.

The contribution of Florence Nightingale to this story is well known, but the nature of her contribution is often misunderstood. Her most lasting impact was not nursing — it was statistics. Her "coxcomb" diagrams, presented to Parliament, showed in vivid visual form that more British soldiers were dying of preventable disease than of battlefield wounds. The diagrams caused a national scandal and led to reforms that saved thousands of lives. She was, in the truest sense, a data visualiser.

These stories share a pattern: the improvements in human lifespan were driven not by dramatic breakthroughs but by systematic attention to the mundane. Clean water. Clean hands. Functioning sewers. Accurate data. The revolution was infrastructural, not miraculous.
