The universe, as far as anyone can tell, does not care how long things last. Stars burn for billions of years. Mayflies get an afternoon. The universe does not appear to have a preference. It simply sets the initial conditions and then wanders off to attend to other galaxies, leaving each species to negotiate its own arrangement with mortality.

For most of the time our species has existed — and by "most" we mean the first hundred thousand years or so, which is roughly ninety-nine per cent of the total, a figure that should give anyone pause about the reliability of recent trends — the arrangement was not generous. The average human could expect to live to about thirty-two. This was not, as is widely believed, because nobody ever saw their fortieth birthday. It is because so many people never saw their fifth. The average was not a description of any individual life. It was a mathematical consequence of the fact that for most of human history, having a child was one of the most dangerous things that could happen to a child.

I believed, until embarrassingly recently, that the number meant what it appeared to mean: that a person in 1900 was elderly at thirty-five, decrepit at forty, and simply not present at fifty. This is the sort of thing that feels true, sounds reasonable, and is precisely, comprehensively wrong. A Roman who survived childhood had a fair chance of reaching sixty. A medieval peasant who made it past five could reasonably expect to see fifty. The thirty-two was not a ceiling. It was an average being hauled violently downward by the deaths of infants, and the difference between those two things is the difference between a civilisation of young deaths and a civilisation of young funerals. Both are tragedies. They are not the same tragedy.

Two-thirds of people, when asked what the global average life expectancy is today, guess too low. They tend to land around sixty, which is where the world was in 1980. This is not unreasonable — it merely means that most people's mental model of the planet is approximately forty-five years out of date, which is, in fairness, the sort of lag that happens when you are busy living on it. The actual figure, as of 2024, is approximately seventy-three years. Humanity, in the space of four generations, added more years to the average lifespan than in all of previous recorded history combined.

To feel what that number means: every single day for the past century, the average human lifespan grew by roughly six hours. While you slept, while you argued about politics, while you waited for the bus and wondered why it was late again, the species was quietly adding another quarter-day to the total. Not because of any single breakthrough. Not because of a genius. Because of pipes, and soap, and a succession of people who thought it might be worth counting the dead and asking why.

The story of how this happened involves, among other things, a Hungarian doctor who was destroyed for being right, a sewer engineer who saved more lives than most physicians, a global campaign to hunt down the last case of humanity's oldest killer, and the single most boring and most important public health intervention in the history of the species. It is, when you look at it closely, the greatest story that nobody tells at parties.

## The Misconception About the Misconception

Before going further, it is worth dwelling on just how layered the misunderstanding is.

Most people believe that life expectancy was thirty-two in 1900, and they picture a world of grey-haired thirty-year-olds, a civilisation that experienced middle age at seventeen and elderliness at about the time a modern person is choosing a degree subject. This is the first misconception, and it is widespread.

The correction — "it was child mortality dragging the average down" — has become common enough that a certain type of person will offer it at dinner parties with visible satisfaction. This is the second misconception: that knowing about the infant mortality effect means you understand it. The actual scale of the thing tends to get lost in the correction.

In 1800, roughly forty-three per cent of children worldwide died before their fifth birthday. Not five per cent. Not ten. Forty-three. Nearly half of all humans born did not make it to the age at which a modern child begins school. In Sweden, which kept unusually good records, the figure was one in three as late as 1800. In parts of Africa, it was higher than one in two well into the twentieth century. These were not rounding errors in the actuarial tables. These were entire populations of people who were expected to exist, planned for, named, loved, and then absent — with such regularity that many cultures did not name children until they had survived their first year, which is not a quaint tradition but a practical adaptation to relentless loss.

Today, global child mortality stands at approximately 3.7 per cent. The drop from forty-three to under four is not a statistical curiosity. It is, in terms of sheer human experience, the largest reduction in routine suffering in the history of the species.

## The Germ Theory Revolution, or: Humanity Discovers the Obvious

For most of recorded history, the dominant medical theory was that disease was caused by bad air. The word "malaria" literally means "bad air." Hospitals were designed with large windows not for the comfort of patients but because physicians believed that if you could get the smell out, the disease would follow. It is worth pausing to appreciate that the entire medical establishment, for centuries, was essentially trying to cure death by improving ventilation.

Ignaz Semmelweis, a Hungarian physician working in Vienna in the 1840s, noticed something peculiar. The maternity ward staffed by doctors had a death rate of ten per cent. The ward staffed by midwives had a rate of two per cent. The difference was that the doctors came directly from performing autopsies — they would dissect a corpse in the morning, rinse their hands in a basin of room-temperature water, and then deliver a baby in the afternoon. Semmelweis hypothesised that they were carrying something from the dead to the living. He introduced hand-washing with chlorinated lime solution. The death rate dropped from ten per cent to roughly one per cent. He had, in essence, discovered that doctors were killing their patients by not washing their hands, which is the kind of finding that tends not to make one popular in the doctors' lounge.

His colleagues responded by having him fired. He was mocked in medical journals. He was stripped of his hospital privileges. He grew increasingly erratic, writing furious open letters to obstetricians across Europe, calling them irresponsible murderers, which had the disadvantage of being both accurate and unhelpful. In 1865, he was committed to an asylum, where he was beaten by guards and died of septicaemia — an infection — at the age of forty-seven. He was killed by the very thing he had spent his life trying to prevent. The universe, as noted, does not care how long things last. It also, apparently, does not care about irony.

His vindication came only after his death, when Louis Pasteur in France and Robert Koch in Germany established germ theory on a firm scientific footing. Joseph Lister, a British surgeon, began sterilising instruments and spraying carbolic acid around operating theatres, reducing surgical mortality so dramatically that his colleagues could no longer pretend the results were a coincidence. They named a mouthwash after Lister, which is not the tribute one imagines he had in mind, but it is the one he got.

## The Most Boring and Most Important Thing That Ever Happened

If you were to rank public health interventions by the number of lives saved versus the excitement they generate in conversation, clean water and sewage systems would be, by several orders of magnitude, the most underappreciated achievement in human history.

In 1858, London experienced what became known as the Great Stink. The Thames, which served as both the city's water supply and its sewer, became so unbearable in a hot summer that Parliament — which sat on the river's bank — could not conduct business. Members held handkerchiefs over their faces. Curtains soaked in chloride of lime were hung in the windows. The government, which had ignored cholera epidemics that killed tens of thousands of working-class Londoners, was moved to action by the fact that the smell had reached the politicians. The democratisation of suffering, it turns out, is an extremely efficient policy mechanism.

Joseph Bazalgette, a retired military engineer, was commissioned to build a sewer system. He was not a doctor. He was not a scientist. He was a man who understood that if sewage went into the same water people drank, people died, and that the solution was not medical but infrastructural. He designed eighty-three miles of brick-lined sewers that intercepted the city's waste before it reached the Thames, and he built them larger than anyone thought necessary because, in his words, "we are only going to do this once." His sewers still function today, which is considerably more than can be said for most things the Victorians built with confidence.

The effect was immediate and staggering. Cholera, which had killed tens of thousands per epidemic, vanished from London. The city's death rate plummeted. Other cities copied the model. By the early twentieth century, the construction of water treatment plants and sewage systems across Europe and North America was saving more lives per year than any medical advance in history. Not with a breakthrough. With pipes.

Modern estimates suggest that clean water and sanitation improvements are responsible for roughly half of the total mortality reduction in the developed world in the first half of the twentieth century. Half. Not antibiotics. Not surgery. Not the discovery of vitamins. Pipes and filtration. It is the kind of finding that makes you want to hug a civil engineer.

## Antibiotics, Vaccines, and the Eradication of an Ancient Killer

The second half of the twentieth century added a new set of tools. Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin in 1928 — which happened because he went on holiday and left a petri dish uncovered, which is not the origin story one expects for a civilisation-altering breakthrough — led to the mass production of antibiotics that turned previously fatal infections into minor inconveniences. Bacterial pneumonia, which had been among the leading killers of adults, became treatable with a course of pills.

Vaccines, meanwhile, were systematically eliminating diseases that had shaped human civilisation for millennia. But the single most extraordinary vaccination story belongs to smallpox, a disease that had killed an estimated three hundred million people in the twentieth century alone — more than all the wars of the century combined.

The World Health Organisation launched its Intensified Smallpox Eradication Programme in 1967, when the disease was still endemic in thirty-three countries. The strategy was simple in concept and monumental in execution: find every case, vaccinate every contact, contain every outbreak. Teams of vaccinators fanned out across South Asia and Africa, often travelling on foot to villages that had never seen a health worker. In Bangladesh, during the war of independence, vaccinators crossed active conflict zones to reach infected patients, because the virus did not observe ceasefire agreements.

The last natural case of smallpox occurred on 26 October 1977, in a hospital cook named Ali Maow Maalin, in the town of Merca, Somalia. Maalin survived. He subsequently became a polio vaccinator, which is the kind of narrative arc that a novelist would reject as too neat. The WHO formally certified the eradication of smallpox in 1980. It remains the only human disease ever deliberately eradicated from the planet. The campaign cost approximately three hundred million dollars in total — less than the world spends on military equipment in a single morning.

## Countries That Transformed

The global average, being a global average, conceals transformations so rapid they look like typographical errors.

Japan in 1945 had a life expectancy of approximately fifty years. The country had just endured two atomic bombings, a devastating war, and widespread malnutrition. By 2024, Japanese life expectancy stood at eighty-five years — the highest in the world. Thirty-five years gained in eighty years. The country went from wartime devastation to global longevity leader in less time than it takes to pay off a mortgage. The mechanisms were not exotic: universal healthcare, excellent nutrition, clean water, strong public health infrastructure, and a culture that considers preventive medicine more interesting than emergency medicine, which is, when you think about it, a remarkably sensible set of priorities.

South Korea's trajectory is, if anything, more dramatic. In 1950, life expectancy was approximately forty-two years. By 2024, it was eighty-four. The entire gap between a pre-industrial and a post-industrial lifespan was closed within a single human lifetime. A Korean grandmother born in the 1940s has lived through a transformation in the basic conditions of human existence that took Europe two centuries.

Bangladesh is the case that should be studied in every public health programme on Earth. In 1971, at independence, life expectancy was forty-seven years. The country was one of the poorest on the planet, routinely described in Western media as a "basket case." By 2024, life expectancy had reached seventy-four years — higher than the global average, and within striking distance of the United States. Bangladesh achieved this without becoming rich. GDP per capita remains a fraction of America's. The gains came from targeted public health interventions: oral rehydration therapy for diarrhoea (slightly salty sugar water, the most important medical advance you have never heard of), widespread vaccination, female education, and the work of organisations like BRAC, which trained village health workers to deliver basic care to communities that had never seen a physician. The lesson of Bangladesh is that you do not need to be wealthy to keep people alive. You need to be organised, and you need to count.

## The COVID Dip and the Recovery

In 2020, global life expectancy dropped by nearly two years — from seventy-three to just over seventy-one. It was the largest single-year decline since the Second World War. The pandemic killed millions directly and disrupted health systems that had been keeping millions more alive through routine care, vaccination programmes, and disease surveillance.

By 2023, the recovery was well underway. Global life expectancy had climbed back to approximately seventy-three, reclaiming most of the lost ground. This is both reassuring and instructive. The underlying infrastructure — the clean water, the sewage systems, the vaccination networks, the trained health workers — did not disappear during the pandemic. It bent. It strained. In some places it broke. But it held, mostly, because the boring foundational work of a century had created systems that could absorb a shock and resume. The recovery was not a miracle. It was a testament to pipes.

## The Gap: Sierra Leone and Japan on the Same Planet

A possiblist holds two things at once, and does not flinch.

A child born in Japan in 2024 can expect to live to eighty-five. A child born in Sierra Leone in 2024 can expect to live to fifty-five. The gap is thirty years. Not thirty years in different centuries. Not thirty years separated by some vast geological or evolutionary divide. Thirty years on the same planet, in the same year, under the same sun.

Thirty years is roughly the entire life expectancy gain that the developed world achieved between 1900 and 1960. It is the distance between a world with widespread child mortality and a world where losing a child is a rare and shattering event rather than a routine one. It is the distance between dying of a treatable infection and not. Between clean water and water that kills. That distance exists right now, between two children born on the same Tuesday, and it is determined almost entirely by the coordinates of the delivery room.

Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for a disproportionate share of this gap. The reasons are layered and interconnected: HIV/AIDS, which reversed life expectancy gains across southern Africa in the 1990s and 2000s; malaria, which still kills over half a million people a year, predominantly children under five; underfunded health systems; conflict; and the compounding effects of poverty on every other determinant of health. The tools to close the gap exist. Clean water technology is not secret. Vaccines are not experimental. Oral rehydration therapy costs pennies. The gap is not a knowledge gap. It is a distribution gap, which is a more uncomfortable diagnosis because it implies that the problem is not what we know but what we have chosen to do with what we know.

Climate change threatens to widen it further. The WHO estimates that between 2030 and 2050, climate change will cause approximately 250,000 additional deaths per year from malnutrition, malaria, diarrhoea, and heat stress — concentrated in the tropical regions that already bear the heaviest burden of preventable death. The cruel arithmetic recurs: the people least responsible for the emissions are the most exposed to the consequences, which is, by any measure of fairness, a distribution problem of a different and more dangerous kind.

## The Quiet Close

The story of human life expectancy is not, ultimately, a story of genius. It is a story of counting. Of people who looked at where the deaths were happening and asked why. Of a sewer engineer who built his pipes larger than necessary because he understood that this was not the sort of thing you get to do twice. Of vaccinators who crossed war zones because the virus did not recognise borders. Of a hospital cook in Somalia who was the last person on Earth to contract a disease that had killed three hundred million in a single century, and who survived, and who then spent the rest of his life vaccinating others.

The gains are real. The inequality is real. The fragility is real. And the most important things about all of it — the pipes, the soap, the salt-and-sugar water, the patient work of counting — are the things least likely to make the evening news, because good infrastructure is, by definition, invisible. You notice the pipe only when it breaks.

Seventy-three years. It was thirty-two within living memory of your great-grandparents. The species doubled its arithmetic, mostly with plumbing.

That is worth knowing.
