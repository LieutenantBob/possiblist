The universe is approximately 13.8 billion years old. For most of that time, nothing lived anywhere. Then, on a small planet orbiting a rather ordinary star, something began to replicate itself, and kept going, and kept going, and went on keeping going with such improbable tenacity that the planet eventually contained more species of beetle than a reasonable designer would have considered strictly necessary. Life, having arrived, proved extraordinarily difficult to get rid of. It survived asteroid impacts, ice ages, volcanic winters, and the invention of the internal combustion engine. It is, at the moment, having a somewhat harder time surviving the invention of the palm oil plantation.

I believed, for longer than I am comfortable admitting, that wildlife was in gentle decline. A slow fade. Something between ten and thirty per cent over a few decades, sad but manageable, the kind of number that lets you nod gravely and change the subject. When Gapminder asked people how much monitored wildlife populations had declined since 1970, forty-eight per cent of respondents underestimated the scale. The most common wrong answer was "about thirty per cent." I would have said the same. Most people imagine a world that is slightly worse than the one their parents knew. The actual number is sixty-nine per cent.

That is not a misprint. It is not a worst-case projection. It is the central estimate from the Living Planet Index, which tracks nearly thirty-two thousand populations of more than five thousand vertebrate species across every continent. Since 1970, the average monitored population has declined by roughly two-thirds. If your bank account had done the same thing, you would have noticed. If a town lost sixty-nine per cent of its population, someone would write a book about it. When the non-human inhabitants of an entire planet do it, it earns a paragraph in a report that almost nobody reads.

The report itself, incidentally, is called the Living Planet Report, which is the kind of title that sounds optimistic until you open it.

What sixty-nine per cent actually means, and what it does not mean, and why the difference matters enormously -- that is where this gets complicated. It is also where it gets important. Because the number is both worse and more nuanced than it first appears, and a possiblist does not get the luxury of picking one.

## What the Number Measures (and What It Does Not)

The Living Planet Index does not say that sixty-nine per cent of all animals are gone. This is the most common misreading and the most forgivable one, because the alternative requires you to understand what a geometric mean of relative population change across a weighted sample of monitored vertebrate populations actually is, and that is not a sentence that has ever kept anyone up at night.

Here is what the LPI actually does. It takes thousands of monitored populations -- a population being a group of the same species in a specific location -- and tracks how each one changes over time. It then calculates the average rate of change across all of them. A sixty-nine per cent decline means that if you set the average at one hundred in 1970, it sits at thirty-one in 2020. It is, in statistical terms, an index, which is the demographer's way of saying "a number that represents a much larger and more complicated number."

This matters because an index can be pulled dramatically by extreme declines in a relatively small number of populations. Brian Leung and colleagues at McGill University published a paper in *Nature* in 2020 arguing that the LPI's catastrophic-looking headline figure is driven disproportionately by a small number of collapsing populations, particularly in Latin America and the Caribbean. Remove the most extreme declines, and the global average looks less apocalyptic, though still deeply concerning. Their alternative analysis suggested a median decline closer to zero -- meaning roughly half of monitored populations were stable or increasing, while the other half were declining, some of them catastrophically.

This does not make the sixty-nine per cent figure wrong. It makes it a specific kind of right. The geometric mean is doing exactly what a geometric mean does: giving heavy weight to severe losses. If you have ten populations and nine are stable but one collapses by ninety-nine per cent, the average tells a story of dramatic decline. Whether that story is "the right" story depends on what question you are trying to answer. If the question is "what is happening on average across monitored wildlife," the LPI is the best instrument we have. If the question is "are most species going extinct," the answer is more complicated.

The distinction between population decline and species extinction is not academic. It is the difference between a wound and a death. A population that has declined by eighty per cent is in serious trouble. It is also still alive. It can, under the right conditions, recover. And some have.

## The Losses That Are Not Ambiguous

Whatever the methodological debate about averages, certain categories of loss are beyond dispute.

Freshwater species have declined by eighty-three per cent. This is the sharpest drop in any major habitat, and it makes a kind of grim sense: rivers and lakes are small, bounded, and directly downstream of everything humans do. You can pollute an ocean and diffuse the damage across a volume of water so large it defies comprehension. Pollute a river and everything in it knows about it by Thursday.

Insects are harder to track -- monitoring programmes for invertebrates are younger and patchier than for vertebrates -- but the data that exists is not reassuring. A 2017 study in Germany found that flying insect biomass had declined by seventy-six per cent over twenty-seven years in protected nature reserves. Not farmland. Not cities. Protected reserves. The insects were vanishing from the places specifically set aside for them, which is rather like discovering that the books are disappearing from the library.

Coral reefs, which support roughly a quarter of all marine species, have lost approximately half their living coral cover since the 1950s. The Great Barrier Reef has experienced five mass bleaching events since 2016. A mass bleaching event is what happens when water temperatures rise enough that corals expel the symbiotic algae that feed them and give them colour, leaving behind white calcium skeletons. It looks, from the air, like a graveyard that does not know it is one yet. Some corals recover. Many do not. The pace of bleaching events is now faster than the recovery time, which is the ecological equivalent of running a deficit on a non-renewable account.

Amphibians may be the most imperilled vertebrate class on Earth. A 2022 assessment found that forty-one per cent of amphibian species are threatened with extinction, driven by a combination of habitat loss, pollution, climate change, and chytrid fungus -- a disease that has been called the most destructive pathogen in recorded history for its breadth of impact across species. The fungus, *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*, has contributed to declines in at least five hundred amphibian species across six continents. It spreads through water. There is no vaccine for a frog.

## The Causes, in Order

The drivers of wildlife decline are not mysterious. They are, in descending order of impact: habitat loss and degradation, overexploitation, climate change, pollution, and invasive species. The proportions vary by region, but the ranking is remarkably stable across analyses.

Habitat loss is responsible for roughly seventy per cent of the decline. This is overwhelmingly driven by agriculture -- the conversion of forest, wetland, and grassland into cropland and pasture. If you had told someone in 1970 that within fifty years we would lose two-thirds of monitored wildlife, they would have assumed something cinematic -- an asteroid, perhaps. Instead it was agriculture and the quiet expansion of human appetite, which turns out to be more effective than any asteroid and harder to dramatize.

Since 1970, the global human population has doubled. Global food production has more than doubled. The amount of land under cultivation has expanded by roughly twelve per cent. Twelve per cent does not sound like much until you consider that it represents an area larger than India, and that the twelve per cent that was converted was, in almost every case, the twelve per cent that was most biologically rich. We did not pave the deserts. We ploughed the forests.

Climate change is an accelerating driver, and its effects compound the others. A species already stressed by habitat loss has less capacity to adapt to shifting temperatures. A coral reef already degraded by pollution bleaches faster under warming seas. The drivers do not operate in isolation. They stack.

## What Has Actually Worked

A possiblist does not stop at the damage report. Not because the damage is not real -- it is -- but because stopping there is its own form of dishonesty. Some things have worked. They deserve to be named.

The great whales are the most dramatic recovery story in modern conservation. Humpback whale populations in the Southern Hemisphere have rebounded from a few hundred individuals to an estimated twenty-five thousand since the 1986 moratorium on commercial whaling. Southern right whales, blue whales, and fin whales have all shown significant recovery. The mechanism was not complicated: stop killing them. The policy innovation required was the international agreement to actually stop, which took roughly forty years of negotiation, several near-collapses of the International Whaling Commission, and the slow realisation that a living whale is worth more to the tourism industry than a dead one is to the rendering plant.

Wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in 1995, after an absence of seventy years. What followed is one of the most studied examples of trophic cascade in ecology. The wolves hunted elk. The elk stopped lingering in river valleys. The vegetation recovered. The rivers changed course. Beavers returned. Songbirds returned. The introduction of thirty-one wolves altered the physical geography of the park. This is not a metaphor. The rivers moved.

Costa Rica went from having one of the highest deforestation rates in the world in the 1980s to being one of the few tropical countries that has substantially increased its forest cover. It did this through a payments-for-ecosystem-services programme that compensated landowners for keeping forests standing. Forest cover has increased from roughly twenty-one per cent in 1987 to over fifty-two per cent today. The programme costs money. It costs less than the alternatives.

In Europe, rewilding efforts have seen the return of European bison to forests in Poland, Romania, and Spain. Beaver populations have expanded across the continent. White-tailed eagle numbers in Germany have increased tenfold since the 1970s. Lynx have been reintroduced to Switzerland, Germany, and France. The European model is not pristine wilderness -- it is managed recovery, coexistence negotiated field by field and farm by farm. It is messy, expensive, and politically fraught. It is also working.

## The Possiblist Position on Bad News

There is a temptation, when writing about wildlife decline for a publication called Possiblist, to find the silver lining and hold it up to the light. To note the whales and the wolves and the Costa Rican forests and suggest that things are, on balance, headed in an acceptable direction.

That would be dishonest.

The recovery stories are real. They are also exceptions against a backdrop of systemic loss. The Living Planet Index is not falling because of a few unlucky species in a few unlucky places. It is falling because the dominant species on the planet has reorganised the surface of the Earth to feed itself, and almost everything else is paying the cost.

A possiblist is not an optimist. Hans Rosling was very clear about this, and he was very clear about it because people kept getting it wrong. A possiblist is someone who looks at all the data -- the trends that are improving and the trends that are not -- and asks what can be done with what is actually true. The sixty-nine per cent decline is actually true. The whale recoveries are also actually true. Holding both of these facts at the same time is not comfortable. It is necessary.

The question is not whether the situation is dire. It is. The question is whether dire situations are necessarily permanent, and the historical record on that point is more interesting than despair would suggest. Every recovery story listed above began with a situation that looked irreversible. The whales were nearly gone. Yellowstone had no wolves. Costa Rica was losing its forests at a rate that extrapolated to bare hillsides within a generation. None of these recoveries happened by accident. They happened because someone decided that the trajectory was not acceptable and then did the specific, expensive, unglamorous work of bending it.

The Living Planet Index does not have to reach zero. That is not a statement of hope. It is a statement about the demonstrated capacity of ecosystems to recover when the pressure is reduced, and the demonstrated capacity of human institutions to reduce the pressure when they decide to. The gap between those two capacities -- between what is possible and what is actually happening -- is where a possiblist lives. It is not a comfortable address. It is an honest one.

The curve bends if we bend it. It does not bend on its own.
