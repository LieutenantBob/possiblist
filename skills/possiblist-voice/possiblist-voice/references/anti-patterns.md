# Anti-Patterns: What Goes Wrong When Writing Like Adams

The most common failure modes when attempting Adams's voice. Each one explains
why it fails and what to do instead.

---

## Anti-Pattern 1: The Laboured Whimsy

**What it looks like**:
> "On this particular Tuesday — Tuesdays being, as everyone knows, the universe's
> least favourite day of the week, for reasons that remain unclear but probably involve
> some kind of cosmic paperwork — humanity was, as usual, getting things rather wrong."

**Why it fails**: The parenthetical is trying to be funny. The trying is visible.
Adams's digressions felt inevitable, not effortful. The joke was never in the digression
itself but in what it set up.

**Fix**: Remove the parenthetical entirely. Earn the digression by making it pay off
later, not by making it quirky in itself.

---

## Anti-Pattern 2: The Smugness

**What it looks like**:
> "Humans, in their infinite capacity for self-deception, have managed to convince
> themselves of something entirely backwards. Of course, we shouldn't be surprised..."

**Why it fails**: "Of course, we shouldn't be surprised" positions the writer outside
and above the humans being described. Adams was never outside. He was in the mess with
everyone else. He was Arthur Dent — not the Guide.

**Fix**: Include the narrator in the confusion. "We" rather than "they." The narrator
is baffled too. Write from inside the species, looking at the species with fond bewilderment.

---

## Anti-Pattern 3: The Announced Punchline

**What it looks like**:
> "Here's the funny thing about this statistic..."
> "And now for the surprising twist..."
> "But wait — it gets better."

**Why it fails**: Adams never announced that a funny thing was coming. The funny thing
arrived. Announcing undermines the joke because the reader is now ready for it. The
comedy depends on the reader not being ready.

**Fix**: Delete the announcement. The funny thing should just be there, in the same
register as the serious thing that preceded it.

---

## Anti-Pattern 4: The Unnecessary Simile

**What it looks like**:
> "The average person's understanding of global poverty is like a map drawn by someone
> who has never left their house."

**Why it fails**: Adams's similes worked because the vehicle of the comparison was
itself surprising ("bricks don't"). A reasonable, illustrative simile is not Adams — it's
just competent writing. The simile should wrong-foot you.

**Fix**: Either make the simile genuinely strange (the comparison should be to something
unexpected that somehow works perfectly) or cut the simile entirely and state the thing
directly.

---

## Anti-Pattern 5: The Overwound Sentence

**What it looks like**:
> "The peculiar and indeed rather remarkable thing about the way in which humans, who
> are after all beings of some considerable intelligence and who have constructed, over
> the course of several millennia, elaborate systems of knowledge-gathering and fact-
> preservation, manage nonetheless, with a consistency that would be admirable if it
> were not quite so alarming, to arrive at conclusions that are not merely wrong but
> wrong in precisely the direction opposite to the truth..."

**Why it fails**: The sentence is long but not tight. Each clause adds length without
building toward a specific landing. An Adams sentence is a mechanism — every clause is
doing structural work toward the punchline. This sentence is just winding.

**Fix**: Identify what the punchline is. Strip the sentence back to only the clauses
that set up that punchline. Make the punchline short.

---

## Anti-Pattern 6: The Missing Resolution

**What it looks like**:
> [A digression about cognitive biases and the availability heuristic and why humans
> tend to over-weight recent memorable events and...]
> [Then immediately: the data, without any bridge]

**Why it fails**: Adams's digressions always paid off. The digression should illuminate
the specific piece of data in a new way. If the digression about cognitive biases doesn't
connect back to the specific misconception being addressed, it's just a digression.

**Fix**: The last sentence of every digression should form an explicit or implicit bridge
back to the central point. It can be oblique. It cannot be absent.

---

## Anti-Pattern 7: The Exclamation Mark

**What it looks like**: Any exclamation mark in any position for any reason.

**Why it fails**: Adams almost never used them. Exclamation marks signal to the reader
that something is exciting or funny. Adams let the thing be exciting or funny on its
own. The exclamation mark is the writer's anxiety about whether the joke worked. Cut it.

**The single exception**: Dialogue, occasionally, when a character is genuinely shouting
or urgently alarmed. Even then, use sparingly.

---

## Anti-Pattern 8: The Moralistic Close

**What it looks like**:
> "And so, if we are to build a better world, we must be willing to question our
> assumptions and seek out the data that challenges our comfortable beliefs."

**Why it fails**: Adams never told you what to do with the information. He trusted that
seeing the world clearly was its own reward. The call-to-action ending transforms a
piece of writing about human bafflement into a self-help article. Adams would find this
both presumptuous and slightly depressing.

**Fix**: End on the observation. Let the reader draw the conclusion. The last sentence
should be a statement of fact or a quiet non-sequitur that resonates, not a directive.

---

## Anti-Pattern 9: The Overcrowded Joke

**What it looks like**:
> "It is the peculiar characteristic of the human species — a species that has built
> cathedrals and symphonies and the Large Hadron Collider and also, somehow, cargo shorts —
> that it manages to be wrong with such exquisite consistency, like a compass that always
> points slightly southeast of magnetic north, which is not exactly north but is at least
> reliably incorrect, which is useful in its own way, like knowing which direction south
> is if you're standing in the right hemisphere and facing the right direction, though at
> this point we may have lost track of what we were talking about."

**Why it fails**: Three jokes competing for the same space. The cargo shorts joke, the
compass joke, and the meta-joke about losing track. Each joke undercuts the others.
Adams's instinct was to commit to one joke per passage and execute it completely.

**Fix**: Pick one. The cargo shorts joke is good. Cut everything from "like a compass"
onward. Let the one thing land.

---

## Anti-Pattern 10: The Tonal Mismatch

**What it looks like**: Starting in Adams voice and then, when delivering the actual data,
switching to a neutral informational tone.

> "On a small blue planet at the unfashionable end of the galaxy, eight billion beings
> are confidently wrong about almost everything. [Adamsian opening, good]
> According to the World Bank's Poverty and Inequality Platform, the global extreme
> poverty rate fell from 36% in 1990 to approximately 10% in 2024. [Data dump, neutral]"

**Why it fails**: The data sounds like it was pasted from a report. The voice evaporates.

**Fix**: The data is delivered *in* the Adams voice. The World Bank is a real institution
and can be named — but the naming should carry the same mild surprise and cosmic
perspective as the rest of the piece. The statistic arrives as if it's slightly
embarrassing for everyone that nobody noticed it before.
