## The Wrong Kind of Wrong
### On humanity's remarkable ability to be pessimistic in entirely the wrong direction

---

Somewhere in the middle third of an unremarkable spiral galaxy — not the glamorous inner
core where all the interesting astrophysics happens, nor the sparse outer edges where
things are merely cold and empty, but the sort of quiet suburban stretch of the Milky
Way that nobody has ever made a documentary about — there exists a small planet whose
dominant species has spent the last several thousand years building an increasingly
sophisticated apparatus for understanding reality, and then, having built it, largely
not checking what it says.

This is not, to be clear, an unusual situation. The universe is almost certainly full of
species that have developed elaborate fact-gathering mechanisms and then formed strong
opinions before consulting them. What makes the inhabitants of this particular planet
notable is the specific direction of their errors. They have not merely got things wrong.
They have got things wrong in a way that requires a certain kind of commitment — a
consistent, species-wide achievement of being wrong in precisely the opposite direction
from the truth.

Take poverty. The world's population has, over the past several decades, been asked in
various surveys what has happened to extreme poverty — the condition of having less than
roughly three dollars a day to survive on, which is not very much, and which the
planet's economists have spent considerable effort trying to measure. The question is
simple: has it got better, worse, or stayed the same?

Fifty-two percent of respondents, across multiple countries and multiple years of
surveys, answered that it had got worse. Roughly another quarter thought it had stayed
about the same. The correct answer — that it had very substantially improved — was
selected by approximately nine percent of people, which is, by a small but meaningful
margin, worse than you would achieve by answering at random.

The chimps, as it were, would have done better.

It would be convenient if this were simply a matter of insufficient information. It
would be tidier if the people who got this wrong had never encountered any data about
global poverty trends, had lived in some kind of hermetically sealed data-free environment,
and had arrived at their incorrect conclusions through the entirely understandable mechanism
of having no information at all. The awkward thing is that most of them had encountered
the information. They had processed it. They had formed a view. The view was wrong, but
it was a view they held with some confidence, which is in its own way rather impressive.

The reason for this — and here we arrive at the only slightly depressing part, though
only slightly — is that the human brain is considerably better at remembering disasters
than it is at remembering gradual improvements. A village destroyed by a flood is a
story. The same village getting steadily better drainage infrastructure over forty years
is not, technically speaking, news. The news therefore contains the flood and not the
drainage, and the brain, receiving a steady diet of floods and no drainage, concludes
that the world is getting worse and that the drainage department is clearly not doing
its job. This is known, in the technical literature, as the availability heuristic,
though in practice it functions less like a heuristic and more like a deeply committed
institutional bias in favour of things going badly.

And things were, for a very long time, going badly. Extreme poverty was not an invention
of economists. In 1990, when the current series of measurements began in earnest, roughly
two billion people were living on the wrong side of the three-dollar-a-day line — a
situation that had been broadly true, in various forms, for most of recorded human
history. The pessimism was, historically speaking, earned. The world had been poor for
a long time. It was not unreasonable to assume it would continue to be poor.

What was, in retrospect, somewhat unreasonable was to continue assuming this after it
stopped being true.

The World Bank's numbers, assembled from household surveys across more than a hundred and
seventy countries, show that the rate of extreme poverty fell from around thirty-six
percent of humanity in 1990 to approximately ten percent today. This represents, in
absolute terms, a reduction of more than a billion and a half people — a figure that
is somewhat difficult to hold in one's head, though one useful way to think about it is
that on any average day over the past thirty-five years, somewhere in the region of a
hundred and fifteen thousand people left extreme poverty behind. Not because they were
rescued by anything in particular. Because economies grew, children went to school,
agricultural productivity improved, and the slow, unspectacular, entirely undramatic
processes of development did what they tend to do when allowed to continue for a
sustained period.

None of this appeared on the news.

There are, to be fair, important things that the number does not say. The progress has
not been evenly distributed. Sub-Saharan Africa — home to an increasing share of the
world's poorest people — has made much less progress than Asia, and in some countries
has gone backwards. The poverty line itself was recently revised upward, adding a
hundred million people to the count in the way that changing a ruler's units changes a
measurement without changing the underlying reality. And the pace of improvement has
slowed since the pandemic, with some of the previous gains proving to be less permanent
than they appeared.

The world is not fixed. But it is different from what most people think it is. It is
different in a specific direction: better.

This is the peculiar situation that humanity finds itself in — holding a pessimistic
view of a world that has, by one important measure, been improving at a pace without
historical precedent, and doing so with considerable confidence. The confidence is
perhaps the most interesting part. It is not the uncertainty of someone who doesn't
know. It is the settled assurance of someone who knows, and knows wrongly, and has no
particular reason to check.

The universe is, on the whole, indifferent to this. The galaxy contains rather a lot
of things to be getting on with, and the specific cognitive biases of one small species
on one small planet are not generally considered one of the more pressing astronomical
concerns.

But it is, for what it's worth, worth knowing.

*Wrong Again is a collection of things the world is doing better at than most people
think, presented with the data to prove it and the mild astonishment it deserves.*
