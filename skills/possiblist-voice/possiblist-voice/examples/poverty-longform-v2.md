## The Wrong Kind of Wrong
### On humanity's rather impressive commitment to pessimism in the face of contrary evidence

---

Somewhere in the quieter reaches of an unremarkable spiral galaxy — not the glamorous
inner core where cosmically interesting things happen, but the kind of neighbourhood
that real estate agents would describe, if they dealt in solar systems, as "up and
coming, with good transport links to the rest of the universe" — a species of
moderately clever primates has spent the last several thousand years constructing
an elaborate apparatus for understanding reality, and has then, with characteristic
thoroughness, largely declined to check what it says.

I should admit, before we go any further, that I was one of these primates. Asked a
few years ago whether global poverty was getting better or worse, I would have said
worse, with the quiet confidence of someone who watches the news and therefore considers
themselves informed. I would have been wrong. In this, at least, I was well accompanied.

Fifty-two percent of people asked this question across multiple countries over multiple
years gave the same answer I would have given. Roughly another quarter said things had
stayed about the same. Nine percent got it right — which is, by a margin small enough
to embarrass everyone involved, worse than selecting an answer at random.

The measurement of global poverty is a relatively recent science. For most of human
history, poverty was so universal as to be effectively invisible — like asking a fish
to measure the wetness of water. It was not until the late 1970s and 1980s that the
World Bank, under the guidance of researchers including the development economist
Martin Ravallion, began systematically assembling household survey data from countries
across the world in a way that allowed global comparisons to be made. What they found,
when they were finally able to look, was that the world was very poor indeed — and, 
more importantly, that it was possible to watch whether it was getting more or less so.

Which brings us, in a reasonably orderly fashion, to now.

Here is the thing that I find genuinely remarkable — and I want to be clear that I am
not performing wonder here, I actually find this wonderful — on any average day over the
past thirty-five years, approximately a hundred and fifteen thousand people left extreme
poverty behind. That is not a typo. Every day. For thirty-five years. If you were to
gather all the people who escaped extreme poverty since 1990 into one place, you would
need to find room for more than a billion and a half people, which is not nothing, and
is in fact the rough combined population of Europe and North America, all of whom would,
one hopes, bring a dish.

The World Bank's Poverty and Inequality Platform — assembled from household surveys
across more than a hundred and seventy countries — puts the global extreme poverty rate
at around thirty-six percent in 1990, and approximately ten percent today. That is not
a modest improvement. That is not a rounding error. The poverty rate, to borrow a phrase
from a genre of writing not usually associated with economic statistics, has very nearly
halved.

Or, rather, it had. The poverty line itself was recently revised — from two dollars and
fifteen cents a day to three dollars — which added approximately a hundred and twenty
five million people to the official count, not because they got poorer, but because the
measuring stick got longer. This is the kind of methodological update that makes
statistics difficult to discuss at dinner parties, and also makes the job of tracking
progress more complicated than a single headline can accommodate.

And here is what the headline cannot accommodate at all: the progress has not been
even. The extraordinary decline in poverty over the past three decades was driven
primarily by Asia — and primarily, within Asia, by China and India, which between them
contain rather a lot of people and rather a lot of economic growth. In Sub-Saharan
Africa, the number of people in extreme poverty has not fallen. It has risen — from
about three hundred and twenty million people in 1990 to nearly six hundred million
today — driven partly by population growth and partly by the stubborn difficulty of
achieving sustained economic development in countries dealing with fragility, conflict,
and the particular kind of bad luck that tends to visit the same places repeatedly.

Why has one part of the world made this progress and another has not? Economists have
thoughts. They are not in complete agreement. This is, as questions go, not yet answered.

What is answered — what is, in fact, rather firmly established — is that the fifty-two
percent had it backwards. The world, or at least the measurable portion of it, is not
getting poorer. It has been getting considerably less poor, at a pace without precedent
in recorded human history, for thirty-five years. This has happened quietly, incrementally,
in ways that do not easily resolve into the kind of story that gets broadcast, and so it
has largely not been broadcast, and so the fifty-two percent have gone on believing the
opposite of what is true.

Somewhere in the middle of an unremarkable galaxy, a species that has built hospitals
and universities and the Large Hadron Collider has also, with considerable commitment,
maintained a comprehensive misunderstanding of how its own world is doing.

It is, as these things go, quite impressive.
