# Misconception Survey Data

Survey data on what people actually believe about the world.
Use these figures for the "What most people think" section of Fact Cards.

Primary source: Gapminder Ignorance Tests (gapminder.org/ignorance)
Conducted by Ipsos MORI for Gapminder Foundation.
Sample: ~30,000 respondents across 28 countries.
Most recent major wave: 2017 (published in Factfulness, 2018).
Follow-up surveys conducted 2020–2023.

---

## Key Survey Results

### Poverty

**Q: In the last 20 years, the proportion of the world population living in extreme poverty has...**
- Almost doubled: 52%
- Stayed the same: 22%  
- Almost halved: 26% ✓ (correct)

*Correct answer: Almost halved. Most people in every country tested were wrong.*

**Q: What percentage of the world's 1-year-old children today have been vaccinated against some disease?**
- 20%: 31%
- 50%: 34%
- 80%: 13% ✓ (approximately correct — actual ~87%)

---

### Education & Gender

**Q: How many years of schooling do women aged 25–34 have on average globally?**
- 7 years ✓ (correct; most people guess much lower)
- Only 13% of respondents answered correctly
- Most common wrong answer: 3 years or fewer

**Q: In all low-income countries across the world today, how many girls finish primary school?**
- 20%: 26%
- 40%: 36%
- 60%: 32% ✓ (approximately correct)

*7 in 10 people underestimate girls' education in low-income countries.*

---

### Health

**Q: What is the life expectancy of the world today?**
- 50 years: 23%
- 60 years: 30%
- 70 years ✓: 47% (actual: ~73 years; 70 counted as correct)

**Q: How many children die before the age of five per 1000 born?**
- Most people overestimate significantly
- Correct range (30–50/1000): guessed by ~20% of respondents
- Most common guess: over 100/1000 (actual global average ~37/1000 in 2023)

---

### Population

**Q: How many people live in the world today?**
- ~7–8 billion: most get this right

**Q: The UN predicts that by 2100 the world population will have increased by another...**
- 3 billion: 24%
- 4 billion: 21%
- 5 billion ✓: 10% (approximately correct)
- Most people underestimate future population growth

**Q: Where does the majority of the world population live?**
- Low-income countries: 20%
- Middle-income countries: 17% ✓ (correct — ~75% of world lives in middle income)
- High-income countries: 63% (WRONG — only ~17% live in high-income countries)

*This is one of the most dramatic misconceptions: most people think most people live in rich countries.*

---

### Climate & Environment

**Q: Climate scientists believe that over the next 100 years, average temperatures will...**
- Get warmer: 86% ✓ (most people get this right)

*Note: climate change is unusual — public belief largely tracks scientific consensus on the basic fact, 
though severity is often underestimated.*

## Country-Level Variation

Most surveys show:
- **Swedes score best** on average (still only ~15% correct on most questions)
- **Americans and Brits** score particularly poorly on poverty-related questions
- **No country's population scores better than random chance** on most questions
- Chimpanzees choosing randomly would score ~33% on 3-option questions; humans average ~10-15%

This is the core Rosling insight: humans systematically get the world wrong, 
in a consistent direction (toward pessimism about progress).

---

## The "Negativity Instinct"

Rosling identified this as the core cognitive bias: news covers bad events, 
not gradual improvements. This creates a systematic illusion that the world is worse than it is.

Key framing for content:
> "Things can be bad AND getting better. The world is not good. But it is better than most people think."

This nuance is important — the goal is not to be Pollyanna, but to be accurate.

## Using This Data in Fact Cards

When writing the "What most people believe" section:
1. If Gapminder survey data exists for this topic: cite it with the % of wrong answers
2. If no survey exists: use hedged language ("most people assume", "the common belief")  
3. Never invent a survey figure — it undermines credibility
4. If possible, use the most relevant country for your primary audience (US/UK figures are often available separately)
